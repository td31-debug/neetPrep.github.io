1 Introduction to Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

NCERT Sec 6.1 & Occurrence

The replacement of one or more hydrogen atoms in an aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbon by halogen atoms (Fluorine, Chlorine, Bromine, or Iodine) results in the formation of haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes (aryl halides), respectively.
• Haloalkanes: Contain halogen atom(s) attached to the sp³ hybridized carbon atom of an alkyl group (General formula: R-X or CnH2n+1X).
• Haloarenes: Contain halogen atom(s) attached directly to sp² hybridized carbon atom(s) of an aromatic benzene ring (General formula: Ar-X).

Haloalkanes and haloarenes find extensive applications in industry and medicine. Many halogen-containing organic compounds occur naturally in plants and microorganisms, while synthetic derivatives serve as indispensable clinical therapeutics and high-efficiency solvents.

💊
Biomedical & Industrial Occurrence (High-Yield NEET Match Questions):
  • Chloramphenicol (Chloromycetin): Chlorine-containing broad-spectrum antibiotic isolated from soil bacterium Streptomyces venezuelae; drug of choice for typhoid fever.
  • Thyroxine: Iodine-containing hormone secreted by the thyroid gland; clinical deficiency results in goitre.
  • Chloroquine: Synthetic chlorine-bearing derivative used for the clinical prophylaxis and treatment of malaria.
  • Halothane (CF₃-CHBrCl): 2-Bromo-2-chloro-1,1,1-trifluoroethane; universally deployed as an inhalation general anaesthetic in surgical procedures.
  • Perfluorocarbons: Fully fluorinated synthetic compounds undergoing investigation as artificial blood substitutes due to exceptional oxygen-dissolving capacity.

2 Classification of Halogen Derivatives

NCERT Sec 6.2 & Hybridisation

Halogen derivatives are systematically classified according to two fundamental criteria: the number of halogen atoms and the hybridisation state of the carbon atom bearing the halogen.

(i) Classification Based on the Number of Halogen Atoms:

Depending on whether one, two, three, or multiple halogen atoms are present:
• Monohalogen compounds: e.g., Ethyl chloride (CH₃CH₂Cl), Chlorobenzene (C₆H₅Cl).
• Dihalogen compounds: Dihaloalkanes are further divided into:
    a) Geminal dihalides (gem-dihalides): Both halogens on the same carbon atom (also termed alkylidene halides, e.g., CH₃CHCl₂ Ethylidene chloride).
    b) Vicinal dihalides (vic-dihalides): Halogens on two adjacent carbon atoms (also termed alkylene dihalides, e.g., CH₂Cl-CH₂Cl Ethylene dichloride).
• Trihalogen compounds: Haloforms like Chloroform (CHCl₃), Iodoform (CHI₃).
• Polyhalogen compounds: Carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄), Hexachlorobenzene.

(ii) Compounds Containing sp³ C-X Bond:

1. Alkyl Halides or Haloalkanes (R-X): Halogen bonded to an sp³ alkyl carbon. Divided into Primary (1°), Secondary (2°), and Tertiary (3°) halides based on the degree of the alpha carbon.
2. Allylic Halides: Halogen atom bonded to an sp³ hybridized carbon adjacent to a carbon-carbon double bond (i.e., at the allylic carbon position).
    Example: CH₂=CH-CH₂-X (Allyl halide) or 3-chlorocyclohex-1-ene.
3. Benzylic Halides: Halogen atom bonded to an sp³ hybridized carbon attached directly to an aromatic benzene ring.
    Example: Benzyl chloride (C₆H₅CH₂Cl, 1°), 1-phenylethyl chloride (2°), and 2-phenyl-2-chloropropane (3°).

(iii) Compounds Containing sp² C-X Bond:

1. Vinylic Halides: Halogen atom bonded directly to an sp² hybridized carbon of a carbon-carbon double bond.
    Example: Vinyl chloride (CH₂=CH-Cl) or 1-bromocyclohex-1-ene.
2. Aryl Halides: Halogen atom bonded directly to an sp² hybridized carbon of an aromatic ring.
    Example: Chlorobenzene (C₆H₅Cl), 2-bromotoluene.

💡
NEET Critical Distinction (Allylic vs Vinylic): In allylic halides (C=C-C-X), the C-X carbon is sp³ and can readily ionize to form a resonance-stabilized allylic carbocation [CH₂=CH-CH₂⁺ ↔ ⁺CH₂-CH=CH₂], making allylic halides extremely reactive towards nucleophilic substitution. In vinylic halides (C=C-X), the C-X carbon is sp² with partial double bond character due to resonance, making it inert towards normal SN reactions!

3 IUPAC Nomenclature of Haloalkanes & Haloarenes

NCERT Rules & Common vs IUPAC

Under standard IUPAC naming guidelines:
• Halogens are always treated as substituent prefixes: fluoro (-F), chloro (-Cl), bromo (-Br), iodo (-I).
• The longest continuous carbon chain containing the halogen is selected as parent.
• Numbering starts from the terminus giving the lowest locant set to substituents.
• Priority rule: If a double or triple bond is present, the multiple bond receives priority over the halogen in determining the lower locant!
• For haloarenes, IUPAC names use the prefix 'halo' before 'benzene' (e.g. Bromobenzene). For disubstituted benzenes, prefixes ortho- (1,2-), meta- (1,3-), and para- (1,4-) are used in common names, while numerals are used in IUPAC.

4 Nature of the Carbon-Halogen (C-X) Bond

Bond Parameters & Dipole Anomaly

Halogen atoms are significantly more electronegative than carbon (Pauling scale: F = 4.0, Cl = 3.0, Br = 2.8, I = 2.5, C = 2.5). Consequently, the shared electron pair of the carbon-halogen bond is drawn towards the halogen atom, rendering carbon with a partial positive charge (δ+) and halogen with a partial negative charge (δ-):
— C^(δ+) — X^(δ-) (Polar Covalent Bond)

Trends in Group 17 Downwards (F → Cl → Br → I):

As the size of the halogen atom increases down the group from fluorine to iodine:
• Bond Length Increases: C-F (139 pm) < C-Cl (178 pm) < C-Br (193 pm) < C-I (214 pm).
• Bond Enthalpy (Strength) Decreases: C-F (452 kJ/mol) > C-Cl (351 kJ/mol) > C-Br (293 kJ/mol) > C-I (234 kJ/mol).
• Reactivity towards Nucleophilic Cleavage Increases: R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F (R-I breaks most readily due to weakest bond and best leaving group ability).

⚡
THE NCERT DIPOLE MOMENT ANOMALY (Repeated NEET Question!): Dipole moment is defined as μ = q × d (charge separation × distance). Although fluorine is the most electronegative element (highest charge separation q), its extremely small atomic radius results in a very short C-F bond distance d. In contrast, the larger C-Cl bond length compensates to yield a higher product.
Experimental Dipole Moments:
• CH₃Cl = 1.860 Debye (HIGHEST)
• CH₃F = 1.847 Debye
• CH₃Br = 1.830 Debye
• CH₃I = 1.636 Debye (LOWEST)
Order of Dipole Moment: CH₃Cl > CH₃F > CH₃Br > CH₃I

5 Preparation of Haloalkanes from Alcohols

Lucas Test & Darzens Process

Alcohols (R-OH) are readily available precursors converted into haloalkanes by replacing the hydroxyl group (-OH) with a halogen atom using three primary chemical routes:

(i) By Reaction with Halogen Acids (HX) & The Lucas Test:

R-OH + HX → R-X + H₂O (in presence of anhydrous ZnCl₂ catalyst).
• Lucas Reagent: An equimolar mixture of concentrated HCl and anhydrous ZnCl₂. ZnCl₂ acts as a Lewis acid coordinator that weakens the C-O bond.
• Reactivity of Alcohols: Tertiary (3°) > Secondary (2°) > Primary (1°). This reactivity order directly mirrors the thermodynamic stability of carbocation intermediates (3° carbocation > 2° carbocation > 1° carbocation).
• Distinction Diagnostic (Lucas Test):
    • 3° Alcohols: Produce immediate dense turbidity at room temperature due to insoluble alkyl chloride.
    • 2° Alcohols: Produce turbidity within ~5 minutes.
    • 1° Alcohols: Do not produce turbidity at room temperature; require prolonged heating.
• Preparation of Alkyl Bromides & Iodides:
    • Alkyl bromides: Heated with 48% HBr or NaBr + conc. H₂SO₄.
    • Alkyl iodides: Heated with KI + 95% H₃PO₄ (phosphoric acid).

⚠️
NCERT Conceptual Trap (Why H₂SO₄ cannot be used with KI): Concentrated sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) is a strong oxidizing agent. When reacted with KI, it produces HI, but immediately oxidizes the formed HI into free Iodine (2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O). Hence, no HI is available to react with alcohol! A non-oxidizing acid like orthophosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) must be used instead.

(ii) By Reaction with Phosphorus Halides:

• With Phosphorus Pentachloride (PCl₅):
R-OH + PCl₅ → R-Cl + POCl₃ (Phosphorus oxychloride) + HCl↑
• With Phosphorus Trichloride (PCl₃):
3R-OH + PCl₃ → 3R-Cl + H₃PO₃ (Phosphorous acid)
• With PBr₃ and PI₃: Since PBr₃ and PI₃ are unstable, they are generated in situ by treating alcohol with Red Phosphorus + Br₂ / I₂.

(iii) By Reaction with Thionyl Chloride (Darzens Process - Best Method):

R-OH + SOCl₂ xrightarrow{text{reflux / pyridine}} R-Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑
Why is Darzens reaction the preferred laboratory method? Both byproducts—Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and Hydrogen chloride (HCl)—are gases that escape into the atmosphere, leaving behind virtually pure alkyl chloride without requiring tedious purification or fractional distillation!

6 Preparation of Haloalkanes from Hydrocarbons

Free Radical, Markovnikov & Peroxide

(i) From Alkanes by Free Radical Halogenation:

Direct chlorination or bromination of alkanes in the presence of ultraviolet light or heat (570-670 K) proceeds via a free radical chain mechanism (Initiation → Propagation → Termination).
CH₄ + Cl₂ xrightarrow{hν} CH₃Cl + CH₂Cl₂ + CHCl₃ + CCl₄ + HCl
• Yields a complex isomeric mixture of mono- and polyhaloalkanes which is difficult to separate.
• Exception: Hydrocarbons where all hydrogens are equivalent give a single monochloro derivative (e.g. Neopentane, C(CH₃)₄ + Cl₂ xrightarrow{hν} (CH₃)₃C-CH₂Cl only 1 isomer!).

(ii) From Alkenes:

1. Addition of Hydrogen Halides (HX) - Markovnikov's Rule:
Electrophilic addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene attaches the negative part (halide ion X⁻) to the carbon atom having the fewer number of hydrogen atoms (due to formation of the more stable carbocation intermediate).
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃-CH(Br)-CH₃ (2-Bromopropane, Major 80%) + CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-Br (Minor 20%)

💥
The Kharasch / Peroxide Effect (Anti-Markovnikov Addition): When HBr ONLY is added to an unsymmetrical alkene in the presence of organic peroxides (e.g. Benzoyl peroxide, (C₆H₅CO)₂O₂), addition takes place opposite to Markovnikov's rule via a free radical chain pathway!
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + HBr xrightarrow{text{Peroxide}} CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-Br (1-Bromopropane, Major 95%)
Crucial NEET Point: Peroxide effect is observed ONLY with HBr. It is NOT exhibited by HF, HCl, or HI!
• For HF: H-F bond is extremely strong; homolytic cleavage is endothermic.
• For HCl: H-Cl bond is too strong for peroxide radicals to abstract chlorine.
• For HI: Iodine radicals are large and combine with each other to form I₂ molecules rather than adding to the double bond!

2. Addition of Halogens (Bromine Water Test for Unsaturation):
Alkenes react with bromine dissolved in carbon tetrachloride (Br₂/CCl₄) to yield vicinal dibromides.
CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ xrightarrow{CCl₄} Br-CH₂-CH₂-Br (1,2-Dibromoethane, colourless)
The characteristic reddish-orange color of bromine is instantly discharged, serving as an indispensable analytical test for carbon-carbon unsaturation.

(iii) From Alkynes:

Alkynes add two molar equivalents of hydrogen halides across the triple bond sequentially to form geminal dihalides:
CH≡CH + HBr → CH₂=CHBr xrightarrow{HBr} CH₃-CHBr₂ (1,1-Dibromoethane)

7 Halogen Exchange & Preparation of Haloarenes

Finkelstein, Swarts & Sandmeyer

Halogen Exchange Reactions:

1. Finkelstein Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Iodides):
Alkyl chlorides or bromides are treated with sodium iodide (NaI) dissolved in dry acetone:
R-Cl / R-Br + NaI xrightarrow{text{dry acetone}} R-I + NaCl↓ / NaBr↓
Driving Force: NaCl and NaBr are insoluble in dry acetone and precipitate out immediately. According to Le Chatelier's principle, their precipitation continuously drives the equilibrium forward!

2. Swarts Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Fluorides):
Heating alkyl chlorides or bromides with heavy metal fluorides such as AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₃, or SbF₃:
CH₃-Br + AgF → CH₃-F + AgBr↓
This is the most efficient and practical method for preparing alkyl fluorides!

(i) Preparation of Haloarenes from Arenes (Electrophilic Substitution):

Aryl chlorides and bromides are prepared by direct electrophilic substitution of arenes with chlorine or bromine in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst (Fe, FeCl₃, or anhydrous AlCl₃) in the dark:
C₆H₅-CH₃ + Cl₂ xrightarrow{Fe text{ / dark}} o-Chlorotoluene + p-Chlorotoluene
The ortho and para isomers are readily separated due to significant differences in their melting points (the symmetrical para isomer packs tighter and has higher m.p.).

(ii) Preparation of Haloarenes from Diazonium Salts:

Primary aromatic amine is diazotized with NaNO₂ + HCl at 273-278 K (0-5 °C) to form benzene diazonium chloride (C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻).
• Sandmeyer Reaction: Freshly prepared diazonium salt is mixed with cuprous chloride (Cu₂Cl₂/HCl) or cuprous bromide (Cu₂Br₂/HBr) to yield chlorobenzene or bromobenzene with evolution of N₂ gas.
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ xrightarrow{Cu₂Cl₂/HCl} C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑
• Gattermann Reaction: Reaction performed using copper powder in presence of halogen acid (Cu/HCl or Cu/HBr). Yield is slightly lower than Sandmeyer.
• Balz-Schiemann Reaction (Fluorobenzene Synthesis): Diazonium salt is treated with fluoroboric acid (HBF₄) to precipitate benzene diazonium fluoroborate, which on dry heating decomposes smoothly to give pure fluorobenzene:
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + HBF₄ → C₆H₅N₂⁺BF₄⁻ xrightarrow{Δ} C₆H₅F + BF₃ + N₂↑
• Synthesis of Iodobenzene: Does not require cuprous halides; simply shaking the diazonium salt solution with aqueous potassium iodide (KI) and warming yields iodobenzene:
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + KI xrightarrow{text{warm}} C₆H₅I + KCl + N₂↑

8 Physical Properties of Haloalkanes

Boiling Points, Density & Solubility

Haloalkanes are colorless when pure (though bromides and iodides develop a yellowish-brown tint upon exposure to light due to photochemical release of free halogen).

1. Melting and Boiling Points:

Haloalkanes possess significantly higher boiling points than parent hydrocarbons of comparable molecular mass because of strong polar dipole-dipole interactions and stronger van der Waals (London dispersion) forces.
• Effect of Halogen Size: For a given alkyl group, boiling point increases with increase in halogen size and polarizability:
R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F
• Effect of Branching (Crucial NEET Trend!): For isomeric haloalkanes, boiling point decreases with increase in chain branching. Branching makes the molecule more compact and spherical, reducing the accessible surface area and thereby weakening van der Waals attraction.
Boiling Points of Isomeric Bromobutanes (C₄H₉Br):
1. CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br (n-Butyl bromide): 375 K (Highest b.p., linear unbranched)
2. (CH₃)₂CH-CH₂-Br (Isobutyl bromide): 364 K
3. CH₃CH₂CH(Br)CH₃ (sec-Butyl bromide): 361 K
4. (CH₃)₃C-Br (tert-Butyl bromide): 346 K (Lowest b.p., spherical compact)
• Isomeric Dihalobenzenes: Boiling points of ortho, meta, and para isomers are nearly identical. However, the melting point of the para isomer is drastically higher (by 70-100 K) than ortho and meta isomers because the symmetrical para isomer fits symmetrically into the crystal lattice!

2. Density:

Density increases with increasing atomic mass of halogen and number of halogen atoms. Monochloroalkanes are lighter than water, but bromoalkanes, iodoalkanes, and polychloro derivatives (like CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄) are denser than water and sink to the bottom.

3. Solubility (NCERT Reasoning Question):

Despite having polar C-X bonds, haloalkanes are virtually insoluble in water.
Reason: To dissolve in water, energy must be supplied to overcome the strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules. The new dipole-dipole attractions established between haloalkane and water molecules release far less energy than required to disrupt the existing hydrogen-bonded network of water. However, haloalkanes dissolve readily in non-polar organic solvents (benzene, ether, chloroform) due to comparable van der Waals interactions.

🎯
AMBIDENT NUCLEOPHILES (100% Guaranteed NEET & CBSE Board Question): Groups containing two nucleophilic centers capable of linking through either of the two atoms are called ambident nucleophiles.

Case 1: Cyanide Ion (CN⁻) vs KCN and AgCN:
• KCN is predominantly ionic: It dissociates completely into K⁺ and [:C≡N:]⁻ in solution. Both carbon and nitrogen have unshared electron pairs. Since a C-C bond is thermodynamically much stronger and more stable than a C-N bond, attack occurs predominantly through the carbon atom, giving Alkyl cyanides (R-CN) as the major product!
• AgCN is predominantly covalent: The Ag-C bond is covalent and the carbon atom is not free. Only the lone pair of electrons on nitrogen is available for nucleophilic donation. Hence attack occurs through nitrogen, producing Alkyl isocyanides (R-NC) as the major product!

Case 2: Nitrite Ion (NO₂⁻) vs KNO₂ and AgNO₂:
• KNO₂ is ionic: Attack occurs through the negatively charged oxygen atom, yielding Alkyl nitrite (R-O-N=O).
• AgNO₂ is covalent: The Ag-O bond is covalent; attack occurs via the lone pair on nitrogen, yielding Nitroalkane (R-NO₂).

10 Stereochemical Aspects & Mechanisms (SN1 vs SN2)

Optical Activity, Walden Inversion & Carbocation

To comprehend nucleophilic substitution mechanisms thoroughly, stereochemical vocabulary must be understood:

Stereochemical Glossary (CBSE Board & NEET Core Definitions):

• Optical Activity: The property of certain chiral substances to rotate the plane of plane-polarized light in a polarimeter. Dextrorotatory (d or +, clockwise) and Laevorotatory (l or -, counter-clockwise).
• Specific Rotation: [α] = α / (l × c), where l is path length in decimeters and c is concentration in g/mL.
• Chiral (Asymmetric) Carbon: A carbon bonded to four entirely different atoms or groups. A molecule lacking an alternating axis of symmetry or plane of symmetry is chiral and non-superimposable on its mirror image (e.g. Butan-2-ol).
• Enantiomers: Stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. They possess identical physical properties (m.p., b.p., density, refractive index) but rotate plane-polarized light by equal magnitudes in opposite directions!
• Diastereomers: Stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other. They exhibit different physical and chemical properties.
• Racemic Mixture (dl-pair or (±)): An equimolar (50:50) mixture of two enantiomers. It has zero net optical rotation due to external compensation (optical rotation of one enantiomer is cancelled by the opposite rotation of the other).
• Resolution: The analytical chemical process of separating a racemic mixture into its individual optically pure enantiomers.
• Racemization: The spontaneous or catalyzed conversion of an optically active enantiomer into an optically inactive racemic mixture.
• Inversion, Retention and Racemization:
    • Retention: Spatial orientation of bonds around the asymmetric center remains preserved.
    • Inversion (Walden Inversion): Absolute spatial arrangement is inverted, like an umbrella turning inside out in a gust of wind!

Mechanisms of Nucleophilic Substitution:

Mechanism A: SN2 (Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular)

• Kinetics: Rate = k [R-X] [Nu⁻] (Second-order kinetics; rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile).
• Number of Steps: Single concerted step without any intermediate.
• Transition State: Pentacoordinate transition state where incoming nucleophile attacks from the back (180° away from the leaving halide). The central carbon is sp² hybridized with three planar equatorial bonds and two partial axial bonds.
• Stereochemical Outcome: Complete 100% Walden Inversion of configuration.
• Reactivity Order (Governed by Steric Hindrance):
CH₃-X > 1° (Primary) > 2° (Secondary) > 3° (Tertiary) 3° alkyl halides are so sterically crowded by bulky methyl groups that backside attack is completely blocked!

Mechanism B: SN1 (Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular)

• Kinetics: Rate = k [R-X] (First-order kinetics; rate depends only on alkyl halide concentration, independent of nucleophile strength).
• Number of Steps: Two distinct steps:
    • Step 1 (Slow, Rate Determining Step): Heterolytic cleavage of C-X bond to form a planar sp² hybridized carbocation intermediate.
    • Step 2 (Fast): Attack of nucleophile from either front or back face of the planar carbocation.
• Stereochemical Outcome: Racemization (with a slight excess of inversion due to partial front-side shielding by the departing halide ion).
• Reactivity Order (Governed by Carbocation Stability):
3° (Tertiary) > 2° (Secondary) > 1° (Primary) > CH₃-X
• Allylic and Benzylic Halides: Undergo SN1 substitution extremely rapidly because the resulting carbocations are resonance-stabilized [C₆H₅CH₂⁺ and CH₂=CH-CH₂⁺]!

11 Elimination Reactions & Organometallic Chemistry

Saytzeff Rule, Grignard & Wurtz

When a haloalkane containing at least one β-hydrogen atom is heated with a strong base such as alcoholic potassium hydroxide (alc. KOH), elimination of hydrogen halide (HX) occurs. Because the hydrogen atom is abstracted from the β-carbon, this reaction is termed β-elimination.

(i) E2 Elimination & The Saytzeff (Zaitsev) Rule:

If the haloalkane possesses two non-equivalent sets of β-hydrogens, dehydrohalogenation can yield isomeric alkenes.
• Saytzeff Rule (Thermodynamic Control): In dehydrohalogenation reactions, the preferred product is the more highly substituted alkene (the alkene carrying the greater number of alkyl substituents attached to the doubly bonded carbons).
Why? More substituted alkenes have a greater number of hyperconjugative α-hydrogens, rendering them thermodynamically more stable.
Example: Dehydrohalogenation of 2-Bromopentane with alc. KOH:
CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH(Br)-CH₃ xrightarrow{text{alc. KOH, } Δ} CH₃-CH₂-CH=CH-CH₃ (Pent-2-ene, 81% Major) + CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH=CH₂ (Pent-1-ene, 19% Minor)

• The Hofmann Rule (Steric Kinetic Control): If a very bulky, sterically hindered base (such as potassium tert-butoxide, t-BuO⁻K⁺) is used, or when the leaving group is fluoride, steric crowding prevents base attack on the internal β-carbon. Base instead abstracts a proton from the more accessible terminal methyl group, yielding the less substituted alkene as major product!

(ii) E1 Elimination:

Unimolecular elimination proceeding via a two-step mechanism with a carbocation intermediate; competes with SN1 at elevated temperatures.

(iii) Reaction with Metals (Grignard Reagent):

Victor Grignard discovered in 1900 that alkyl halides react with magnesium metal shavings in dry ether to form alkyl magnesium halides (Grignard Reagents, R-Mg-X).
R-X + Mg xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} R-Mg-X
• In Grignard reagents, the carbon-magnesium bond is covalent but highly polar (carbon is electronegative relative to Mg and bears a partial negative charge C^(δ-) — Mg^(δ+)), making the alkyl group a potent carbanionic nucleophile and extremely strong base!
• Anhydrous Conditions Mandatory: Grignard reagents react furiously with any source of active protons (water, alcohols, amines, carboxylic acids) to form hydrocarbons:
R-Mg-X + H₂O → R-H (Alkane) + Mg(OH)X
Even traces of ambient moisture destroy the Grignard reagent, which is why absolute anhydrous dry ether is strictly required!

(iv) Reduction of Haloalkanes:

• R-X + Zn / HCl → R-H + ZnCl₂
• R-X + LiAlH₄ → R-H (effective for 1° and 2° halides; 3° halides give predominantly elimination).
• R-X + Red P + HI xrightarrow{423 text{ K}} R-H + I₂

(v) Wurtz Reaction:

Alkyl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to yield symmetrical alkanes containing double the number of carbon atoms:
2R-X + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} R-R + 2NaX
Example: 2CH₃CH₂Br + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ (n-Butane) + 2NaBr
• Limitation: Cross-Wurtz reaction between two different alkyl halides (R-X + R'-X) produces a mixture of three alkanes (R-R, R'-R', and R-R'), which have very close boiling points and cannot be cleanly separated. Furthermore, methane cannot be synthesized by Wurtz reaction!

12 Reactions of Haloarenes

Dow's Process, EAS & DDT Synthesis

(i) Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution (SNAr) & Relative Inertness:

Aryl halides are extremely unreactive towards nucleophilic substitution compared to alkyl halides. NCERT delineates four key physical reasons:
1. Resonance Effect: The lone pair electrons on halogen delocalize into the aromatic π-electron cloud, creating partial double-bond character in the C-Cl bond:
The C-Cl bond length in chlorobenzene is only 169 pm, significantly shorter and stronger than the pure single C-Cl bond in chloroethane (178 pm), making it far harder to cleave!
2. Difference in Hybridisation of Carbon Atom: In haloalkanes, carbon is sp³ hybridized (25% s-character), whereas in haloarenes, carbon is sp² hybridized (33% s-character). Greater s-character holds bonding electrons closer to the nucleus, strengthening the bond.
3. Instability of Phenyl Cation: If ionization occurs, the phenyl cation (C₆H₅⁺) cannot be stabilized by resonance because the vacant sp² orbital is perpendicular to the aromatic π-orbitals.
4. Electronic Repulsion: The approaching electron-rich nucleophile is strongly repelled by the high π-electron density of the benzene ring.

• Dow's Process (Industrial Phenol Production): Substitution requires extreme drastic conditions:
C₆H₅Cl + NaOH (6-8%) xrightarrow{623 text{ K}, 300 text{ atm}} C₆H₅O⁻Na⁺ xrightarrow{H⁺} C₆H₅OH (Phenol)

🔬
Activation by Electron Withdrawing Groups (-NO₂): Introducing strong electron withdrawing groups (-NO₂) at ortho and para positions dramatically accelerates nucleophilic substitution:
• p-Nitrochlorobenzene: Reacts with aqueous NaOH at 443 K.
• 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene: Reacts with warm aqueous Na₂CO₃ at 368 K.
• 2,4,6-Trinitrochlorobenzene (Picryl chloride): Simply warming with water at 323 K (50 °C) yields Picric acid!
Why Ortho and Para only? The intermediate carbanion (Meisenheimer complex) places the delocalized negative charge precisely on the ortho and para carbon atoms. The nitro group at these positions directly stabilizes the negative charge via strong -M and -I effects. At meta positions, no resonance contributor places negative charge on the meta carbon!

(ii) Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) of Haloarenes:

Halogen atoms are ortho, para-directing due to resonance (+M effect) which increases electron density at ortho and para positions. However, due to their strong electronegativity (-I effect), they are overall deactivating. Reactions are slower than in benzene and require stronger Lewis acid catalysts:
• Halogenation: C₆H₅Cl + Cl₂ xrightarrow{text{anh. } FeCl₃} 1,4-Dichlorobenzene (Major) + 1,2-Dichlorobenzene (Minor)
• Nitration: C₆H₅Cl + conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ → 1-Chloro-4-nitrobenzene (Major) + 1-Chloro-2-nitrobenzene (Minor)
• Sulphonation: C₆H₅Cl + conc. H₂SO₄ xrightarrow{Δ} 4-Chlorobenzenesulphonic acid (Major)
• Friedel-Crafts Alkylation: C₆H₅Cl + CH₃Cl xrightarrow{text{anh. } AlCl₃} 1-Chloro-4-methylbenzene (Major)
• Friedel-Crafts Acylation: C₆H₅Cl + CH₃COCl xrightarrow{text{anh. } AlCl₃} 4-Chloroacetophenone (Major)

(iii) Reactions with Metals:

• Fittig Reaction: Aryl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to yield diphenyl (biphenyl):
2C₆H₅Br + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} C₆H₅-C₆H₅ (Biphenyl) + 2NaBr
• Wurtz-Fittig Reaction: A mixture of an aryl halide and an alkyl halide treated with sodium in dry ether gives an alkylarene:
C₆H₅Br + CH₃Br + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} C₆H₅-CH₃ (Toluene) + 2NaBr

(iv) Reaction with Chloral (Synthesis of DDT):

When chloral (trichloroacetaldehyde, CCl₃CHO) is heated with two moles of chlorobenzene in the presence of concentrated sulphuric acid:
CCl₃-CHO + 2 C₆H₅Cl xrightarrow{text{conc. } H₂SO₄} (p-Cl-C₆H₄)₂CH-CCl₃ (DDT) + H₂O
Product: p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT).

13 Polyhalogen Compounds

NCERT High-Yield Recall & Ecology

Carbon compounds containing more than one halogen atom are termed polyhalogen compounds, having critical pharmaceutical, refrigeration, and environmental significance:

(i) Dihalides:

• Methylene Chloride (Dichloromethane, CH₂Cl₂): Industrial solvent for paint stripping, aerosol propellant, and solvent in antibiotic manufacturing. Low-level exposure impairs hearing and vision; high exposure damages central nervous system.

(ii) Trihalogen Derivatives:

• Chloroform (Trichloromethane, CHCl₃): Historically used as an inhalation anaesthetic.

⚠️
CHLOROFORM STORAGE RULE & PHOSGENE (Classic Board & NEET Question): Chloroform is slowly oxidized by atmospheric oxygen in the presence of sunlight into an extremely poisonous gas called Phosgene (Carbonyl chloride, COCl₂):
2CHCl₃ + O₂ xrightarrow{text{light}} 2COCl₂ (Phosgene) + 2HCl
Safety Protocol: Chloroform must be stored in tightly closed, dark brown or amber bottles filled to the very brim to completely exclude air. Additionally, 1% ethanol is added as a stabilizer:
Ethanol converts any trace of toxic phosgene that might form into harmless, non-toxic diethyl carbonate:
COCl₂ + 2C₂H₅OH → (C₂H₅O)₂C=O (Diethyl carbonate) + 2HCl

• Iodoform (Triiodomethane, CHI₃): Yellow crystalline solid with a distinct antiseptic medicinal odor.
Antiseptic action: Due to the gradual liberation of free elemental iodine, not iodoform itself!
Iodoform Test: Diagnostic test for compounds containing CH₃-C=O or CH₃-CH(OH)- groups when warmed with I₂ + NaOH, yielding yellow precipitate of CHI₃ (m.p. 392 K).

(iii) Tetrahalogen Derivatives:

• Carbon Tetrachloride (Tetrachloromethane, CCl₄):
Manufactured by chlorination of methane or carbon disulphide (CS₂ + 3Cl₂ → CCl₄ + S₂Cl₂).
Used as a fire extinguisher under the trade name Pyrene (dense, non-combustible vapors smother the flame). Exposure causes liver cancer, dizziness, and stratospheric ozone depletion.

(iv) Freons:

Chlorofluorocarbon (CFC) derivatives of methane and ethane are termed Freons.
• Freon-12 (Dichlorodifluoromethane, CCl₂F₂): Manufactured from CCl₄ via the Swarts reaction:
CCl₄ + 2AgF / SbF₃ → CCl₂F₂ (Freon-12) + 2AgCl
• Properties: Colorless, odorless, non-toxic, non-corrosive, easily liquefiable gas.
• Environmental Impact: Extremely stable in the troposphere; diffuses into the stratosphere where UV radiation breaks it down to release chlorine free radicals (Cl•), which catalytically destroy the protective stratospheric ozone layer.

(v) DDT (p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane):

• First chlorinated modern synthetic insecticide, discovered by Paul Müller in 1939 (Nobel Prize in Medicine, 1948).
• Systematic Name: 2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)-1,1,1-trichloroethane.
• Ecological Hazard: Highly fat-soluble and resistant to biodegradation. Accumulates along food chains (biomagnification), disrupting calcium metabolism in birds and thinning eggshell membranes. Banned globally under the Stockholm Convention.

1 Introduction to Carbonyl Compounds

Theory & Occurrence

Carbonyl compounds are organic substances containing the carbon-oxygen double bond (>C=O), which constitutes one of the most vital functional groups in organic and biological chemistry.

In aldehydes, the carbonyl group is bonded to at least one hydrogen atom and one alkyl or aryl group (general formula R-CHO, with formaldehyde HCHO having two hydrogens). In ketones, the carbonyl carbon is bonded to two carbon substituents (general formula R-CO-R'), which may be aliphatic or aromatic.

🌿
Natural Occurrence & Fragrances (High-Yield NEET Recall):
  • Vanillin: 4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde, extracted from vanilla beans.
  • Salicylaldehyde: 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde, found in meadowsweet.
  • Cinnamaldehyde: 3-phenylprop-2-enal, extracted from cinnamon bark.
  • Acetophenone: Naturally present in apples, bananas, and apricots.

Carbonyl compounds play a foundational role in living organisms. Carbohydrates (aldoses and ketoses), nucleic acids, microbial pheromones, and steroid hormones (progesterone, cortisone, testosterone) all contain carbonyl groups. Industrially, they are manufactured in millions of tonnes annually as solvents, polymer precursors (e.g. Bakelite), adhesives, and pharmaceuticals.

2 Nomenclature and Structure of Carbonyl Group

IUPAC & Bonding

Under IUPAC nomenclature:
• For open-chain aldehydes, the suffix '-al' replaces the terminal '-e' of the parent alkane. The carbonyl carbon is always numbered as C-1.
• For cyclic aldehydes where the -CHO group is directly attached to a ring, the suffix 'carbaldehyde' is appended to the full name of the cycloalkane (e.g. Cyclohexanecarbaldehyde).
• For ketones, the suffix '-one' replaces the terminal '-e' of the alkane, with the carbonyl position designated by the lowest locant (e.g. Pentan-2-one).

Electronic Structure & Polar Nature of >C=O

The carbonyl carbon is sp² hybridized and forms three coplanar σ-bonds directed towards the corners of an equilateral triangle with bond angles of approximately 120°. The fourth valence electron resides in an unhybridized 2p orbital perpendicular to the σ-plane, overlapping sideways with an oxygen 2p orbital to form a π-bond.

Resonance in Carbonyl Group: >C=O ⟷ >C⁺—O⁻ (Electrophilic Cδ⁺ / Nucleophilic Oδ⁻)
🎯
Key Examination Insights (VSEPR & Dipole Moment):
• The C=O bond length is 1.23 Å, significantly shorter than the C-O single bond (1.43 Å).
• Oxygen has electronegativity 3.5 versus carbon's 2.5. This ΔEN = 1.0 creates a large permanent dipole moment (μ = 2.3 to 2.8 D).
• Consequently, carbonyl compounds are substantially more polar than isomeric ethers (μ ≈ 1.15 D).

3 Preparation of Aldehydes and Ketones

Reactions & Mechanisms

Preparation methods are divided into general methods (producing both) and exclusive specialized syntheses.

A. General Methods

1. Controlled Oxidation of Alcohols:
• R-CH₂OH + PCC (Pyridinium chlorochromate in CH₂Cl₂) ⟶ R-CHO (Stops at aldehyde!)
• R-CH(OH)-R' + CrO₃ (or K₂Cr₂O₇ / H⁺) ⟶ R-CO-R' (Ketone)
2. Catalytic Dehydrogenation over Heated Copper at 573 K:
• 1° Alcohol: R-CH₂OH ⟶[Cu, 573 K] R-CHO + H₂↑
• 2° Alcohol: R-CH(OH)-R' ⟶[Cu, 573 K] R-CO-R' + H₂↑
• 3° Alcohol (NEET TRAP!): Dehydrates to Alkene instead! ((CH₃)₃C-OH ⟶ (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ + H₂O)
3. Kucherov Hydration of Alkynes (HgSO₄ / H₂SO₄ at 333 K):
• Ethyne: HC≡CH + H₂O ⟶[Hg²⁺ / H⁺, 333 K] [CH₂=CH-OH] ⟶ CH₃CHO (Only ethyne yields aldehyde!)
• Propyne: CH₃-C≡CH + H₂O ⟶[Markovnikov] [CH₃-C(OH)=CH₂] ⟶ CH₃COCH₃ (Acetone)

B. Exclusive Syntheses for Aldehydes

1. Rosenmund Reduction:
R-COCl + H₂ ⟶[Pd - BaSO₄ / Quinoline] R-CHO + HCl
(BaSO₄ poisons the Pd catalyst, halting reduction before alcohol formation)
2. Stephen Reaction & DIBAL-H:
• R-C≡N + SnCl₂ + HCl ⟶ R-CH=NH·HCl ⟶[H₃O⁺] R-CHO
• R-C≡N ⟶[1. DIBAL-H (195 K) / 2. H₂O] R-CHO (Ester: R-COOR' ⟶ R-CHO)
3. Etard Reaction (Toluene to Benzaldehyde):
C₆H₅-CH₃ + 2 CrO₂Cl₂ (in CS₂) ⟶ Brown Chromium Complex ⟶[H₃O⁺] C₆H₅-CHO
4. Gattermann-Koch Reaction:
Benzene + CO + HCl ⟶[anh. AlCl₃ / CuCl] Benzaldehyde

C. Exclusive Syntheses for Ketones

1. From Dialkylcadmium with Acyl Chlorides:
2 R-COCl + R'₂Cd ⟶ 2 R-CO-R' + CdCl₂
(R'₂Cd is less nucleophilic than Grignard, preventing addition to the product ketone!)
2. From Nitriles with Grignard Reagent:
CH₃-C≡N + C₆H₅MgBr ⟶[ether] CH₃-C(=NMgBr)-C₆H₅ ⟶[H₃O⁺] C₆H₅-CO-CH₃ (Acetophenone)

4 Physical Properties of Aldehydes and Ketones

Boiling Points & Solubility

1. Physical State & Odour:
• Methanal is a pungent gas at room temperature (b.p. 252 K).
• Ethanal is a volatile liquid boiling at 294 K (21°C).
• Other aldehydes and ketones containing up to 11 carbons are colourless liquids; higher members are solids.
• Lower aldehydes possess sharp pungent odours. As molecular size increases, odour becomes less pungent and more fragrant/fruity (widely used in perfumery).

2. Boiling Point Comparisons (High-Yield Order):
Carbonyl compounds have dipole-dipole attractions because of the polar C=O group. They lack intermolecular hydrogen bonding with themselves (unlike alcohols and carboxylic acids).

🌡️
NEET Standard Boiling Point Hierarchy (Comparable Molecular Weight ≈ 58 - 60 g/mol):
Carboxylic Acids (CH₃COOH: 118°C) > Alcohols (CH₃CH₂CH₂OH: 97°C) > Ketones (CH₃COCH₃: 56°C) > Aldehydes (CH₃CH₂CHO: 49°C) > Ethers (C₂H₅OCH₃: 11°C) > Alkanes (n-Butane: -0.5°C).

3. Solubility in Water:
Lower aldehydes and ketones (Methanal, Ethanal, Propanone) are miscible with water in all proportions because they form intermolecular hydrogen bonds with water molecules through their carbonyl oxygen:
>C=O ··· H—O—H
Solubility drops sharply as alkyl chain length increases beyond 4 carbons, because the non-polar hydrophobic hydrocarbon chain overpowers the polar interaction.

5 Chemical Reactions & Uses of Aldehydes and Ketones

Distinction Tests & Reactions

A. Nucleophilic Addition & Reactivity Order

Aldehydes are generally much more reactive than ketones towards nucleophilic attack due to:
1. Steric effect: Two alkyl groups in ketones crowd the transition state more than one alkyl group in aldehydes.
2. Electronic (+I) effect: Two electron-releasing alkyl groups in ketones reduce the partial positive charge (δ⁺) on carbonyl carbon more effectively.

Relative Reactivity: HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃CH₂CHO > CH₃COCH₃ > C₆H₅CHO > C₆H₅COCH₃

B. NEET Distinction Tests

1. Tollens' Test (Silver Mirror Test):
R-CHO + 2 [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3 OH⁻ ⟶ R-COO⁻ + 2 Ag↓ (Silver Mirror) + 4 NH₃ + 2 H₂O
(Positive for ALL aldehydes; negative for ketones)
2. Fehling's Test:
R-CHO + 2 Cu²⁺ + 5 OH⁻ ⟶ R-COO⁻ + Cu₂O↓ (Red-Brown Ppt) + 3 H₂O
(Positive for ALIPHATIC aldehydes only! Aromatic aldehydes like Benzaldehyde DO NOT react!)
3. Iodoform Reaction (Haloform Test):
CH₃-CO-R + 3 I₂ + 4 NaOH ⟶ R-COONa + CHI₃↓ (Yellow Ppt, m.p. 119°C) + 3 NaI + 3 H₂O
(Positive for all methyl ketones and acetaldehyde CH₃CHO, plus CH₃-CH(OH)- alcohols)

C. Aldol Condensation vs Cannizzaro Reaction

• Aldol Condensation: Aldehydes/ketones having at least one α-hydrogen heated with dilute alkali (dil. NaOH) form β-hydroxyaldehydes (aldol), which upon heating lose H₂O to form α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds (e.g. But-2-enal from ethanal).
• Cannizzaro Reaction: Aldehydes having no α-hydrogen (HCHO, C₆H₅CHO, (CH₃)₃C-CHO) undergo disproportionation (self oxidation-reduction) with concentrated alkali (50% NaOH) to give one molecule of alcohol and one molecule of carboxylic acid salt.

D. Prominent Industrial & Medical Uses

• Formalin: 40% aqueous solution of formaldehyde, widely used to preserve biological specimens and embalm tissue.
• Bakelite: Formed by condensation polymerization of phenol and formaldehyde.
• Acetone: Foremost laboratory and industrial solvent for cellulose acetate, nitrocellulose, lacquers, and varnishes.
• Benzaldehyde: Known as oil of bitter almonds, used in perfumery, cosmetics, and dye synthesis.

6 Carboxylic Acids: Structure and Nomenclature

Resonance & Bonding

Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl functional group (-COOH), comprised of a carbonyl group (>C=O) attached directly to a hydroxyl group (-OH).

Resonance of the Carboxyl Group

Unlike simple carbonyl compounds, the lone pair of electrons on the hydroxyl oxygen is delocalized into the carbonyl π-system:

R-C(=O)-OH ⟷ R-C(-O⁻)=O⁺H

Consequence for Reactivity: This resonance delocalization reduces the partial positive charge on the carbonyl carbon. Therefore, the carbonyl carbon in carboxylic acids is much less electrophilic than in aldehydes and ketones, and carboxylic acids do not undergo nucleophilic addition reactions characteristic of aldehydes and ketones.

7 Methods for Preparation of Carboxylic Acids

Synthetic Routes

Carboxylic acids can be synthesized through multiple reliable laboratory and industrial routes:

1. Oxidation of 1° Alcohols & Aldehydes:
R-CH₂OH ⟶[alkaline KMnO₄ or Jones reagent CrO₃ / H₂SO₄] R-COOH
2. Oxidation of Alkylbenzenes (Vigorous KMnO₄ Oxidation):
C₆H₅-CH₃ (or C₆H₅-CH₂CH₃) ⟶[KMnO₄ - KOH, Δ] C₆H₅-COOK ⟶[H₃O⁺] C₆H₅-COOH
(NEET Rule: Any alkyl group with at least one benzylic hydrogen oxidizes to -COOH. tert-Butylbenzene fails to react!)
3. Hydrolysis of Nitriles and Amides:
R-C≡N ⟶[H⁺ or OH⁻, H₂O] R-CONH₂ (Amide) ⟶[H₃O⁺, Δ] R-COOH + NH₄⁺
4. Grignard Reagent with Dry Ice (Carbon Dioxide):
R-MgX + O=C=O ⟶[dry ether] R-COOMgX ⟶[H₃O⁺] R-COOH + Mg(OH)X
(Extremely useful method for ascending the carbon chain by one carbon atom!)
5. Hydrolysis of Acyl Chlorides, Anhydrides, and Esters:
• R-COCl + H₂O ⟶ R-COOH + HCl
• R-COOR' + H₂O ⇌[H⁺] R-COOH + R'-OH (or Saponification with NaOH)

8 Physical Properties of Carboxylic Acids

Dimerization & B.P.

1. Unusually High Boiling Points:
Carboxylic acids boil at significantly higher temperatures than alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, or ethers of comparable molecular mass. For example, ethanoic acid (M = 60) boils at 118°C, while propan-1-ol (M = 60) boils at 97°C.

🔗
Explanation of Stable Cyclic Dimerization:
In carboxylic acids, the hydroxyl group is strongly polarized by the adjacent carbonyl oxygen. Two molecules of carboxylic acid form an exceptionally stable 8-membered cyclic dimer held by two intermolecular hydrogen bonds.
• These dimers persist even in the vapour phase and in non-polar solvents like benzene.
• In benzene, the measured molecular mass of acetic acid is 120 g/mol (double the formula mass of 60 g/mol!).

2. Water Solubility:
Aliphatic carboxylic acids having up to 4 carbon atoms (methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic, butanoic) are miscible with water in all proportions because of strong hydrogen bonding with water molecules.
Solubility diminishes rapidly for higher carboxylic acids due to the increasing size of the hydrophobic hydrocarbon portion. Benzoic acid is practically insoluble in cold water.

9 Chemical Properties & Acidity of Carboxylic Acids

Acidity, HVZ & Decarboxylation

A. Acidity and Carboxylate Ion Resonance

Carboxylic acids are the most acidic simple organic compounds. They react with active metals (Na, K) to liberate H₂, with strong bases (NaOH) to form salts, and with weak bases like NaHCO₃ to generate brisk effervescence of CO₂ gas:

R-COOH + NaHCO₃ ⟶ R-COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑ (Brisk Effervescence)

📌 Crucial NEET Distinction: Phenols DO NOT liberate CO₂ from aqueous NaHCO₃ because phenols are weaker acids (pKa ≈ 10) than carbonic acid (pKa ≈ 6.4). Carboxylic acids (pKa ≈ 4-5) are stronger than carbonic acid.

Why is Carboxylate Ion More Stable than Phenoxide Ion?

In carboxylate ion (RCOO⁻), the negative charge is delocalized over two identical highly electronegative oxygen atoms (bond length 1.27 Å, bond order 1.5). In phenoxide ion, the negative charge is delocalized onto less electronegative carbon atoms of the benzene ring. Hence, carboxylate is far more stable, making carboxylic acids much stronger acids than phenols!

⚖️
Effect of Substituents on Acidity (Decreasing Order of -I / -M Effect):
-CF₃ > -NO₂ > -CN > -F > -Cl > -Br > -I > -Ph
• Electron-withdrawing groups disperse negative charge ⟶ stabilize RCOO⁻ ⟶ Increase acidity (lower pKa).
• Electron-donating (+I) alkyl groups intensify negative charge ⟶ Decrease acidity.
CF₃COOH > CCl₃COOH > CHCl₂COOH > NO₂CH₂COOH > FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > HCOOH > C₆H₅COOH > CH₃COOH

B. Reactions Involving C-OH Cleavage

1. Anhydride Formation: 2 CH₃COOH ⟶[P₂O₅, Δ] (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O
2. Esterification (Fischer): R-COOH + R'-OH ⇌[H₂SO₄] R-COOR' + H₂O
3. Acid Chlorides: R-COOH + SOCl₂ ⟶ R-COCl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑ (SOCl₂ preferred as byproducts are gases!)
4. With Ammonia: CH₃COOH + NH₃ ⇌ CH₃COONH₄ ⟶[Δ, -H₂O] CH₃CONH₂ (Acetamide)

C. Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky (HVZ) & Decarboxylation

1. Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky (HVZ) Reaction:
R-CH₂-COOH + Br₂ ⟶[Red P / H₂O] R-CH(Br)-COOH (α-Bromocarboxylic acid)
(Requires at least one α-hydrogen atom! Formic acid & Benzoic acid do NOT undergo HVZ!)
2. Soda-Lime Decarboxylation:
R-COONa + NaOH ⟶[CaO, Δ] R-H (Alkane with one less carbon) + Na₂CO₃
3. Aromatic Electrophilic Ring Substitution:
-COOH is meta-directing and deactivating. Nitration gives m-nitrobenzoic acid.
(NEET TRAP: Benzoic acid does NOT undergo Friedel-Crafts alkylation or acylation because the Lewis acid AlCl₃ coordinates with the -COOH group!)
