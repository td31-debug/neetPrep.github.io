1 Introduction to Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

NCERT Sec 6.1 & Occurrence

The replacement of one or more hydrogen atoms in an aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbon by halogen atoms (Fluorine, Chlorine, Bromine, or Iodine) results in the formation of haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes (aryl halides), respectively.
• Haloalkanes: Contain halogen atom(s) attached to the sp³ hybridized carbon atom of an alkyl group (General formula: R-X or CnH2n+1X).
• Haloarenes: Contain halogen atom(s) attached directly to sp² hybridized carbon atom(s) of an aromatic benzene ring (General formula: Ar-X).

Haloalkanes and haloarenes find extensive applications in industry and medicine. Many halogen-containing organic compounds occur naturally in plants and microorganisms, while synthetic derivatives serve as indispensable clinical therapeutics and high-efficiency solvents.

💊
Biomedical & Industrial Occurrence (High-Yield NEET Match Questions):
  • Chloramphenicol (Chloromycetin): Chlorine-containing broad-spectrum antibiotic isolated from soil bacterium Streptomyces venezuelae; drug of choice for typhoid fever.
  • Thyroxine: Iodine-containing hormone secreted by the thyroid gland; clinical deficiency results in goitre.
  • Chloroquine: Synthetic chlorine-bearing derivative used for the clinical prophylaxis and treatment of malaria.
  • Halothane (CF₃-CHBrCl): 2-Bromo-2-chloro-1,1,1-trifluoroethane; universally deployed as an inhalation general anaesthetic in surgical procedures.
  • Perfluorocarbons: Fully fluorinated synthetic compounds undergoing investigation as artificial blood substitutes due to exceptional oxygen-dissolving capacity.

2 Classification of Halogen Derivatives

NCERT Sec 6.2 & Hybridisation

Halogen derivatives are systematically classified according to two fundamental criteria: the number of halogen atoms and the hybridisation state of the carbon atom bearing the halogen.

(i) Classification Based on the Number of Halogen Atoms:

Depending on whether one, two, three, or multiple halogen atoms are present:
• Monohalogen compounds: e.g., Ethyl chloride (CH₃CH₂Cl), Chlorobenzene (C₆H₅Cl).
• Dihalogen compounds: Dihaloalkanes are further divided into:
    a) Geminal dihalides (gem-dihalides): Both halogens on the same carbon atom (also termed alkylidene halides, e.g., CH₃CHCl₂ Ethylidene chloride).
    b) Vicinal dihalides (vic-dihalides): Halogens on two adjacent carbon atoms (also termed alkylene dihalides, e.g., CH₂Cl-CH₂Cl Ethylene dichloride).
• Trihalogen compounds: Haloforms like Chloroform (CHCl₃), Iodoform (CHI₃).
• Polyhalogen compounds: Carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄), Hexachlorobenzene.

(ii) Compounds Containing sp³ C-X Bond:

1. Alkyl Halides or Haloalkanes (R-X): Halogen bonded to an sp³ alkyl carbon. Divided into Primary (1°), Secondary (2°), and Tertiary (3°) halides based on the degree of the alpha carbon.
2. Allylic Halides: Halogen atom bonded to an sp³ hybridized carbon adjacent to a carbon-carbon double bond (i.e., at the allylic carbon position).
    Example: CH₂=CH-CH₂-X (Allyl halide) or 3-chlorocyclohex-1-ene.
3. Benzylic Halides: Halogen atom bonded to an sp³ hybridized carbon attached directly to an aromatic benzene ring.
    Example: Benzyl chloride (C₆H₅CH₂Cl, 1°), 1-phenylethyl chloride (2°), and 2-phenyl-2-chloropropane (3°).

(iii) Compounds Containing sp² C-X Bond:

1. Vinylic Halides: Halogen atom bonded directly to an sp² hybridized carbon of a carbon-carbon double bond.
    Example: Vinyl chloride (CH₂=CH-Cl) or 1-bromocyclohex-1-ene.
2. Aryl Halides: Halogen atom bonded directly to an sp² hybridized carbon of an aromatic ring.
    Example: Chlorobenzene (C₆H₅Cl), 2-bromotoluene.

💡
NEET Critical Distinction (Allylic vs Vinylic): In allylic halides (C=C-C-X), the C-X carbon is sp³ and can readily ionize to form a resonance-stabilized allylic carbocation [CH₂=CH-CH₂⁺ ↔ ⁺CH₂-CH=CH₂], making allylic halides extremely reactive towards nucleophilic substitution. In vinylic halides (C=C-X), the C-X carbon is sp² with partial double bond character due to resonance, making it inert towards normal SN reactions!

3 IUPAC Nomenclature of Haloalkanes & Haloarenes

NCERT Rules & Common vs IUPAC

Under standard IUPAC naming guidelines:
• Halogens are always treated as substituent prefixes: fluoro (-F), chloro (-Cl), bromo (-Br), iodo (-I).
• The longest continuous carbon chain containing the halogen is selected as parent.
• Numbering starts from the terminus giving the lowest locant set to substituents.
• Priority rule: If a double or triple bond is present, the multiple bond receives priority over the halogen in determining the lower locant!
• For haloarenes, IUPAC names use the prefix 'halo' before 'benzene' (e.g. Bromobenzene). For disubstituted benzenes, prefixes ortho- (1,2-), meta- (1,3-), and para- (1,4-) are used in common names, while numerals are used in IUPAC.

4 Nature of the Carbon-Halogen (C-X) Bond

Bond Parameters & Dipole Anomaly

Halogen atoms are significantly more electronegative than carbon (Pauling scale: F = 4.0, Cl = 3.0, Br = 2.8, I = 2.5, C = 2.5). Consequently, the shared electron pair of the carbon-halogen bond is drawn towards the halogen atom, rendering carbon with a partial positive charge (δ+) and halogen with a partial negative charge (δ-):
— C^(δ+) — X^(δ-) (Polar Covalent Bond)

Trends in Group 17 Downwards (F → Cl → Br → I):

As the size of the halogen atom increases down the group from fluorine to iodine:
• Bond Length Increases: C-F (139 pm) < C-Cl (178 pm) < C-Br (193 pm) < C-I (214 pm).
• Bond Enthalpy (Strength) Decreases: C-F (452 kJ/mol) > C-Cl (351 kJ/mol) > C-Br (293 kJ/mol) > C-I (234 kJ/mol).
• Reactivity towards Nucleophilic Cleavage Increases: R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F (R-I breaks most readily due to weakest bond and best leaving group ability).

⚡
THE NCERT DIPOLE MOMENT ANOMALY (Repeated NEET Question!): Dipole moment is defined as μ = q × d (charge separation × distance). Although fluorine is the most electronegative element (highest charge separation q), its extremely small atomic radius results in a very short C-F bond distance d. In contrast, the larger C-Cl bond length compensates to yield a higher product.
Experimental Dipole Moments:
• CH₃Cl = 1.860 Debye (HIGHEST)
• CH₃F = 1.847 Debye
• CH₃Br = 1.830 Debye
• CH₃I = 1.636 Debye (LOWEST)
Order of Dipole Moment: CH₃Cl > CH₃F > CH₃Br > CH₃I

5 Preparation of Haloalkanes from Alcohols

Lucas Test & Darzens Process

Alcohols (R-OH) are readily available precursors converted into haloalkanes by replacing the hydroxyl group (-OH) with a halogen atom using three primary chemical routes:

(i) By Reaction with Halogen Acids (HX) & The Lucas Test:

R-OH + HX → R-X + H₂O (in presence of anhydrous ZnCl₂ catalyst).
• Lucas Reagent: An equimolar mixture of concentrated HCl and anhydrous ZnCl₂. ZnCl₂ acts as a Lewis acid coordinator that weakens the C-O bond.
• Reactivity of Alcohols: Tertiary (3°) > Secondary (2°) > Primary (1°). This reactivity order directly mirrors the thermodynamic stability of carbocation intermediates (3° carbocation > 2° carbocation > 1° carbocation).
• Distinction Diagnostic (Lucas Test):
    • 3° Alcohols: Produce immediate dense turbidity at room temperature due to insoluble alkyl chloride.
    • 2° Alcohols: Produce turbidity within ~5 minutes.
    • 1° Alcohols: Do not produce turbidity at room temperature; require prolonged heating.
• Preparation of Alkyl Bromides & Iodides:
    • Alkyl bromides: Heated with 48% HBr or NaBr + conc. H₂SO₄.
    • Alkyl iodides: Heated with KI + 95% H₃PO₄ (phosphoric acid).

⚠️
NCERT Conceptual Trap (Why H₂SO₄ cannot be used with KI): Concentrated sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) is a strong oxidizing agent. When reacted with KI, it produces HI, but immediately oxidizes the formed HI into free Iodine (2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O). Hence, no HI is available to react with alcohol! A non-oxidizing acid like orthophosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) must be used instead.

(ii) By Reaction with Phosphorus Halides:

• With Phosphorus Pentachloride (PCl₅):
R-OH + PCl₅ → R-Cl + POCl₃ (Phosphorus oxychloride) + HCl↑
• With Phosphorus Trichloride (PCl₃):
3R-OH + PCl₃ → 3R-Cl + H₃PO₃ (Phosphorous acid)
• With PBr₃ and PI₃: Since PBr₃ and PI₃ are unstable, they are generated in situ by treating alcohol with Red Phosphorus + Br₂ / I₂.

(iii) By Reaction with Thionyl Chloride (Darzens Process - Best Method):

R-OH + SOCl₂ xrightarrow{text{reflux / pyridine}} R-Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑
Why is Darzens reaction the preferred laboratory method? Both byproducts—Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and Hydrogen chloride (HCl)—are gases that escape into the atmosphere, leaving behind virtually pure alkyl chloride without requiring tedious purification or fractional distillation!

6 Preparation of Haloalkanes from Hydrocarbons

Free Radical, Markovnikov & Peroxide

(i) From Alkanes by Free Radical Halogenation:

Direct chlorination or bromination of alkanes in the presence of ultraviolet light or heat (570-670 K) proceeds via a free radical chain mechanism (Initiation → Propagation → Termination).
CH₄ + Cl₂ xrightarrow{hν} CH₃Cl + CH₂Cl₂ + CHCl₃ + CCl₄ + HCl
• Yields a complex isomeric mixture of mono- and polyhaloalkanes which is difficult to separate.
• Exception: Hydrocarbons where all hydrogens are equivalent give a single monochloro derivative (e.g. Neopentane, C(CH₃)₄ + Cl₂ xrightarrow{hν} (CH₃)₃C-CH₂Cl only 1 isomer!).

(ii) From Alkenes:

1. Addition of Hydrogen Halides (HX) - Markovnikov's Rule:
Electrophilic addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene attaches the negative part (halide ion X⁻) to the carbon atom having the fewer number of hydrogen atoms (due to formation of the more stable carbocation intermediate).
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃-CH(Br)-CH₃ (2-Bromopropane, Major 80%) + CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-Br (Minor 20%)

💥
The Kharasch / Peroxide Effect (Anti-Markovnikov Addition): When HBr ONLY is added to an unsymmetrical alkene in the presence of organic peroxides (e.g. Benzoyl peroxide, (C₆H₅CO)₂O₂), addition takes place opposite to Markovnikov's rule via a free radical chain pathway!
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + HBr xrightarrow{text{Peroxide}} CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-Br (1-Bromopropane, Major 95%)
Crucial NEET Point: Peroxide effect is observed ONLY with HBr. It is NOT exhibited by HF, HCl, or HI!
• For HF: H-F bond is extremely strong; homolytic cleavage is endothermic.
• For HCl: H-Cl bond is too strong for peroxide radicals to abstract chlorine.
• For HI: Iodine radicals are large and combine with each other to form I₂ molecules rather than adding to the double bond!

2. Addition of Halogens (Bromine Water Test for Unsaturation):
Alkenes react with bromine dissolved in carbon tetrachloride (Br₂/CCl₄) to yield vicinal dibromides.
CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ xrightarrow{CCl₄} Br-CH₂-CH₂-Br (1,2-Dibromoethane, colourless)
The characteristic reddish-orange color of bromine is instantly discharged, serving as an indispensable analytical test for carbon-carbon unsaturation.

(iii) From Alkynes:

Alkynes add two molar equivalents of hydrogen halides across the triple bond sequentially to form geminal dihalides:
CH≡CH + HBr → CH₂=CHBr xrightarrow{HBr} CH₃-CHBr₂ (1,1-Dibromoethane)

7 Halogen Exchange & Preparation of Haloarenes

Finkelstein, Swarts & Sandmeyer

Halogen Exchange Reactions:

1. Finkelstein Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Iodides):
Alkyl chlorides or bromides are treated with sodium iodide (NaI) dissolved in dry acetone:
R-Cl / R-Br + NaI xrightarrow{text{dry acetone}} R-I + NaCl↓ / NaBr↓
Driving Force: NaCl and NaBr are insoluble in dry acetone and precipitate out immediately. According to Le Chatelier's principle, their precipitation continuously drives the equilibrium forward!

2. Swarts Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Fluorides):
Heating alkyl chlorides or bromides with heavy metal fluorides such as AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₃, or SbF₃:
CH₃-Br + AgF → CH₃-F + AgBr↓
This is the most efficient and practical method for preparing alkyl fluorides!

(i) Preparation of Haloarenes from Arenes (Electrophilic Substitution):

Aryl chlorides and bromides are prepared by direct electrophilic substitution of arenes with chlorine or bromine in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst (Fe, FeCl₃, or anhydrous AlCl₃) in the dark:
C₆H₅-CH₃ + Cl₂ xrightarrow{Fe text{ / dark}} o-Chlorotoluene + p-Chlorotoluene
The ortho and para isomers are readily separated due to significant differences in their melting points (the symmetrical para isomer packs tighter and has higher m.p.).

(ii) Preparation of Haloarenes from Diazonium Salts:

Primary aromatic amine is diazotized with NaNO₂ + HCl at 273-278 K (0-5 °C) to form benzene diazonium chloride (C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻).
• Sandmeyer Reaction: Freshly prepared diazonium salt is mixed with cuprous chloride (Cu₂Cl₂/HCl) or cuprous bromide (Cu₂Br₂/HBr) to yield chlorobenzene or bromobenzene with evolution of N₂ gas.
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ xrightarrow{Cu₂Cl₂/HCl} C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑
• Gattermann Reaction: Reaction performed using copper powder in presence of halogen acid (Cu/HCl or Cu/HBr). Yield is slightly lower than Sandmeyer.
• Balz-Schiemann Reaction (Fluorobenzene Synthesis): Diazonium salt is treated with fluoroboric acid (HBF₄) to precipitate benzene diazonium fluoroborate, which on dry heating decomposes smoothly to give pure fluorobenzene:
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + HBF₄ → C₆H₅N₂⁺BF₄⁻ xrightarrow{Δ} C₆H₅F + BF₃ + N₂↑
• Synthesis of Iodobenzene: Does not require cuprous halides; simply shaking the diazonium salt solution with aqueous potassium iodide (KI) and warming yields iodobenzene:
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + KI xrightarrow{text{warm}} C₆H₅I + KCl + N₂↑

8 Physical Properties of Haloalkanes

Boiling Points, Density & Solubility

Haloalkanes are colorless when pure (though bromides and iodides develop a yellowish-brown tint upon exposure to light due to photochemical release of free halogen).

1. Melting and Boiling Points:

Haloalkanes possess significantly higher boiling points than parent hydrocarbons of comparable molecular mass because of strong polar dipole-dipole interactions and stronger van der Waals (London dispersion) forces.
• Effect of Halogen Size: For a given alkyl group, boiling point increases with increase in halogen size and polarizability:
R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F
• Effect of Branching (Crucial NEET Trend!): For isomeric haloalkanes, boiling point decreases with increase in chain branching. Branching makes the molecule more compact and spherical, reducing the accessible surface area and thereby weakening van der Waals attraction.
Boiling Points of Isomeric Bromobutanes (C₄H₉Br):
1. CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br (n-Butyl bromide): 375 K (Highest b.p., linear unbranched)
2. (CH₃)₂CH-CH₂-Br (Isobutyl bromide): 364 K
3. CH₃CH₂CH(Br)CH₃ (sec-Butyl bromide): 361 K
4. (CH₃)₃C-Br (tert-Butyl bromide): 346 K (Lowest b.p., spherical compact)
• Isomeric Dihalobenzenes: Boiling points of ortho, meta, and para isomers are nearly identical. However, the melting point of the para isomer is drastically higher (by 70-100 K) than ortho and meta isomers because the symmetrical para isomer fits symmetrically into the crystal lattice!

2. Density:

Density increases with increasing atomic mass of halogen and number of halogen atoms. Monochloroalkanes are lighter than water, but bromoalkanes, iodoalkanes, and polychloro derivatives (like CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄) are denser than water and sink to the bottom.

3. Solubility (NCERT Reasoning Question):

Despite having polar C-X bonds, haloalkanes are virtually insoluble in water.
Reason: To dissolve in water, energy must be supplied to overcome the strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules. The new dipole-dipole attractions established between haloalkane and water molecules release far less energy than required to disrupt the existing hydrogen-bonded network of water. However, haloalkanes dissolve readily in non-polar organic solvents (benzene, ether, chloroform) due to comparable van der Waals interactions.

10 Stereochemical Aspects & Mechanisms (SN1 vs SN2)

Optical Activity, Walden Inversion & Carbocation

To comprehend nucleophilic substitution mechanisms thoroughly, stereochemical vocabulary must be understood:

Stereochemical Glossary (CBSE Board & NEET Core Definitions):

• Optical Activity: The property of certain chiral substances to rotate the plane of plane-polarized light in a polarimeter. Dextrorotatory (d or +, clockwise) and Laevorotatory (l or -, counter-clockwise).
• Specific Rotation: [α] = α / (l × c), where l is path length in decimeters and c is concentration in g/mL.
• Chiral (Asymmetric) Carbon: A carbon bonded to four entirely different atoms or groups. A molecule lacking an alternating axis of symmetry or plane of symmetry is chiral and non-superimposable on its mirror image (e.g. Butan-2-ol).
• Enantiomers: Stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. They possess identical physical properties (m.p., b.p., density, refractive index) but rotate plane-polarized light by equal magnitudes in opposite directions!
• Diastereomers: Stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other. They exhibit different physical and chemical properties.
• Racemic Mixture (dl-pair or (±)): An equimolar (50:50) mixture of two enantiomers. It has zero net optical rotation due to external compensation (optical rotation of one enantiomer is cancelled by the opposite rotation of the other).
• Resolution: The analytical chemical process of separating a racemic mixture into its individual optically pure enantiomers.
• Racemization: The spontaneous or catalyzed conversion of an optically active enantiomer into an optically inactive racemic mixture.
• Inversion, Retention and Racemization:
    • Retention: Spatial orientation of bonds around the asymmetric center remains preserved.
    • Inversion (Walden Inversion): Absolute spatial arrangement is inverted, like an umbrella turning inside out in a gust of wind!

Mechanisms of Nucleophilic Substitution:

Mechanism A: SN2 (Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular)

• Kinetics: Rate = k [R-X] [Nu⁻] (Second-order kinetics; rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile).
• Number of Steps: Single concerted step without any intermediate.
• Transition State: Pentacoordinate transition state where incoming nucleophile attacks from the back (180° away from the leaving halide). The central carbon is sp² hybridized with three planar equatorial bonds and two partial axial bonds.
• Stereochemical Outcome: Complete 100% Walden Inversion of configuration.
• Reactivity Order (Governed by Steric Hindrance):
CH₃-X > 1° (Primary) > 2° (Secondary) > 3° (Tertiary) 3° alkyl halides are so sterically crowded by bulky methyl groups that backside attack is completely blocked!

Mechanism B: SN1 (Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular)

• Kinetics: Rate = k [R-X] (First-order kinetics; rate depends only on alkyl halide concentration, independent of nucleophile strength).
• Number of Steps: Two distinct steps:
    • Step 1 (Slow, Rate Determining Step): Heterolytic cleavage of C-X bond to form a planar sp² hybridized carbocation intermediate.
    • Step 2 (Fast): Attack of nucleophile from either front or back face of the planar carbocation.
• Stereochemical Outcome: Racemization (with a slight excess of inversion due to partial front-side shielding by the departing halide ion).
• Reactivity Order (Governed by Carbocation Stability):
3° (Tertiary) > 2° (Secondary) > 1° (Primary) > CH₃-X
• Allylic and Benzylic Halides: Undergo SN1 substitution extremely rapidly because the resulting carbocations are resonance-stabilized [C₆H₅CH₂⁺ and CH₂=CH-CH₂⁺]!

11 Elimination Reactions & Organometallic Chemistry

Saytzeff Rule, Grignard & Wurtz

When a haloalkane containing at least one β-hydrogen atom is heated with a strong base such as alcoholic potassium hydroxide (alc. KOH), elimination of hydrogen halide (HX) occurs. Because the hydrogen atom is abstracted from the β-carbon, this reaction is termed β-elimination.

(i) E2 Elimination & The Saytzeff (Zaitsev) Rule:

If the haloalkane possesses two non-equivalent sets of β-hydrogens, dehydrohalogenation can yield isomeric alkenes.
• Saytzeff Rule (Thermodynamic Control): In dehydrohalogenation reactions, the preferred product is the more highly substituted alkene (the alkene carrying the greater number of alkyl substituents attached to the doubly bonded carbons).
Why? More substituted alkenes have a greater number of hyperconjugative α-hydrogens, rendering them thermodynamically more stable.
Example: Dehydrohalogenation of 2-Bromopentane with alc. KOH:
CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH(Br)-CH₃ xrightarrow{text{alc. KOH, } Δ} CH₃-CH₂-CH=CH-CH₃ (Pent-2-ene, 81% Major) + CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH=CH₂ (Pent-1-ene, 19% Minor)

• The Hofmann Rule (Steric Kinetic Control): If a very bulky, sterically hindered base (such as potassium tert-butoxide, t-BuO⁻K⁺) is used, or when the leaving group is fluoride, steric crowding prevents base attack on the internal β-carbon. Base instead abstracts a proton from the more accessible terminal methyl group, yielding the less substituted alkene as major product!

(ii) E1 Elimination:

Unimolecular elimination proceeding via a two-step mechanism with a carbocation intermediate; competes with SN1 at elevated temperatures.

(iii) Reaction with Metals (Grignard Reagent):

Victor Grignard discovered in 1900 that alkyl halides react with magnesium metal shavings in dry ether to form alkyl magnesium halides (Grignard Reagents, R-Mg-X).
R-X + Mg xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} R-Mg-X
• In Grignard reagents, the carbon-magnesium bond is covalent but highly polar (carbon is electronegative relative to Mg and bears a partial negative charge C^(δ-) — Mg^(δ+)), making the alkyl group a potent carbanionic nucleophile and extremely strong base!
• Anhydrous Conditions Mandatory: Grignard reagents react furiously with any source of active protons (water, alcohols, amines, carboxylic acids) to form hydrocarbons:
R-Mg-X + H₂O → R-H (Alkane) + Mg(OH)X
Even traces of ambient moisture destroy the Grignard reagent, which is why absolute anhydrous dry ether is strictly required!

(iv) Reduction of Haloalkanes:

• R-X + Zn / HCl → R-H + ZnCl₂
• R-X + LiAlH₄ → R-H (effective for 1° and 2° halides; 3° halides give predominantly elimination).
• R-X + Red P + HI xrightarrow{423 text{ K}} R-H + I₂

(v) Wurtz Reaction:

Alkyl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to yield symmetrical alkanes containing double the number of carbon atoms:
2R-X + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} R-R + 2NaX
Example: 2CH₃CH₂Br + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ (n-Butane) + 2NaBr
• Limitation: Cross-Wurtz reaction between two different alkyl halides (R-X + R'-X) produces a mixture of three alkanes (R-R, R'-R', and R-R'), which have very close boiling points and cannot be cleanly separated. Furthermore, methane cannot be synthesized by Wurtz reaction!

12 Reactions of Haloarenes

Dow's Process, EAS & DDT Synthesis

(i) Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution (SNAr) & Relative Inertness:

Aryl halides are extremely unreactive towards nucleophilic substitution compared to alkyl halides. NCERT delineates four key physical reasons:
1. Resonance Effect: The lone pair electrons on halogen delocalize into the aromatic π-electron cloud, creating partial double-bond character in the C-Cl bond:
The C-Cl bond length in chlorobenzene is only 169 pm, significantly shorter and stronger than the pure single C-Cl bond in chloroethane (178 pm), making it far harder to cleave!
2. Difference in Hybridisation of Carbon Atom: In haloalkanes, carbon is sp³ hybridized (25% s-character), whereas in haloarenes, carbon is sp² hybridized (33% s-character). Greater s-character holds bonding electrons closer to the nucleus, strengthening the bond.
3. Instability of Phenyl Cation: If ionization occurs, the phenyl cation (C₆H₅⁺) cannot be stabilized by resonance because the vacant sp² orbital is perpendicular to the aromatic π-orbitals.
4. Electronic Repulsion: The approaching electron-rich nucleophile is strongly repelled by the high π-electron density of the benzene ring.

• Dow's Process (Industrial Phenol Production): Substitution requires extreme drastic conditions:
C₆H₅Cl + NaOH (6-8%) xrightarrow{623 text{ K}, 300 text{ atm}} C₆H₅O⁻Na⁺ xrightarrow{H⁺} C₆H₅OH (Phenol)

🔬
Activation by Electron Withdrawing Groups (-NO₂): Introducing strong electron withdrawing groups (-NO₂) at ortho and para positions dramatically accelerates nucleophilic substitution:
• p-Nitrochlorobenzene: Reacts with aqueous NaOH at 443 K.
• 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene: Reacts with warm aqueous Na₂CO₃ at 368 K.
• 2,4,6-Trinitrochlorobenzene (Picryl chloride): Simply warming with water at 323 K (50 °C) yields Picric acid!
Why Ortho and Para only? The intermediate carbanion (Meisenheimer complex) places the delocalized negative charge precisely on the ortho and para carbon atoms. The nitro group at these positions directly stabilizes the negative charge via strong -M and -I effects. At meta positions, no resonance contributor places negative charge on the meta carbon!

(ii) Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) of Haloarenes:

Halogen atoms are ortho, para-directing due to resonance (+M effect) which increases electron density at ortho and para positions. However, due to their strong electronegativity (-I effect), they are overall deactivating. Reactions are slower than in benzene and require stronger Lewis acid catalysts:
• Halogenation: C₆H₅Cl + Cl₂ xrightarrow{text{anh. } FeCl₃} 1,4-Dichlorobenzene (Major) + 1,2-Dichlorobenzene (Minor)
• Nitration: C₆H₅Cl + conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ → 1-Chloro-4-nitrobenzene (Major) + 1-Chloro-2-nitrobenzene (Minor)
• Sulphonation: C₆H₅Cl + conc. H₂SO₄ xrightarrow{Δ} 4-Chlorobenzenesulphonic acid (Major)
• Friedel-Crafts Alkylation: C₆H₅Cl + CH₃Cl xrightarrow{text{anh. } AlCl₃} 1-Chloro-4-methylbenzene (Major)
• Friedel-Crafts Acylation: C₆H₅Cl + CH₃COCl xrightarrow{text{anh. } AlCl₃} 4-Chloroacetophenone (Major)

(iii) Reactions with Metals:

• Fittig Reaction: Aryl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to yield diphenyl (biphenyl):
2C₆H₅Br + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} C₆H₅-C₆H₅ (Biphenyl) + 2NaBr
• Wurtz-Fittig Reaction: A mixture of an aryl halide and an alkyl halide treated with sodium in dry ether gives an alkylarene:
C₆H₅Br + CH₃Br + 2Na xrightarrow{text{dry ether}} C₆H₅-CH₃ (Toluene) + 2NaBr

When chloral (trichloroacetaldehyde, CCl₃CHO) is heated with two moles of chlorobenzene in the presence of concentrated sulphuric acid:
CCl₃-CHO + 2 C₆H₅Cl xrightarrow{text{conc. } H₂SO₄} (p-Cl-C₆H₄)₂CH-CCl₃ (DDT) + H₂O

1 Classification of Alcohols

NCERT Sec 7.1 & Monohydric to Polyhydric

Alcohols are hydroxy derivatives of aliphatic hydrocarbons in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by hydroxyl (-OH) groups.

A. Based on Number of Hydroxyl Groups:

• Monohydric Alcohols: Contain one -OH group (e.g., Ethyl alcohol, CH₃CH₂OH).
• Dihydric Alcohols (Glycols): Contain two -OH groups on adjacent carbons (e.g., Ethane-1,2-diol / Ethylene glycol, CH₂OH-CH₂OH).
• Trihydric Alcohols (Glycerols): Contain three -OH groups (e.g., Propane-1,2,3-triol / Glycerol, CH₂OH-CHOH-CH₂OH).
• Polyhydric Alcohols: Contain four or more -OH groups (e.g., Sorbitol, Mannitol).

B. Based on Degree of Carbon Bearing –OH:

• Primary (1°) Alcohol: -OH attached to a primary carbon (-CH₂OH, e.g., Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH).
• Secondary (2°) Alcohol: -OH attached to a secondary carbon (>CHOH, e.g., Propan-2-ol (CH₃)₂CHOH).
• Tertiary (3°) Alcohol: -OH attached to a tertiary carbon (-COH, e.g., 2-Methylpropan-2-ol (CH₃)₃COH).
• Allylic & Benzylic Alcohols: Can be 1°, 2°, or 3° based on substitution at the allylic or benzylic sp³ carbon.

3 Structure of the Hydroxyl Group in Alcohols

Orbital Geometry & Polar Bonds

In alcohols, the oxygen atom of the -OH group is attached to carbon by a sigma (σ) bond formed by the overlap of an sp³ hybridized orbital of carbon with an sp³ hybridized orbital of oxygen.
• Bond Angle: In methanol (CH₃OH), the C–O–H bond angle is 108.9°, slightly less than regular tetrahedral angle (109.5°) due to repulsive interactions between the two unshared lone pairs on oxygen.
• Bond Polarities: Both C–O and O–H bonds are strongly polarized because of high electronegativity of oxygen (Pauling 3.5 vs C 2.5 and H 2.1).

📐
Bond Angle Contrast (Methanol vs Dimethyl Ether):
• In Methanol (CH₃-OH): Bond angle is 108.9° (smaller than tetrahedral due to lone-pair repulsion).
• In Dimethyl Ether (CH₃-O-CH₃): Bond angle is 111.7° (GREATER than tetrahedral!) due to strong repulsive steric crowding between the two bulky methyl groups!

5 Physical Properties of Alcohols

Intermolecular H-Bonding & Boiling Points

• Boiling Points: Alcohols have much higher boiling points than alkanes, haloalkanes, and ethers of comparable molecular masses because alcohols associate through strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
• Effect of Chain Length: Boiling points increase regularly with increase in the number of carbon atoms due to increase in surface area and van der Waals forces.
• Effect of Branching: Among isomeric alcohols, boiling point decreases with branching: 1° Alcohol > 2° Alcohol > 3° Alcohol (spherical shape decreases surface area).
• Solubility in Water: Lower alcohols are completely miscible with water in all proportions because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. Solubility decreases as the hydrophobic alkyl chain becomes larger (beyond C₄).

6 Chemical Reactions of Alcohols

O–H vs C–O Cleavage & Dehydration

A. Cleavage of O–H Bond (Acidity of Alcohols):

• Reaction with active metals: Alcohols react with Na, K, Al to liberate hydrogen gas and form metal alkoxides:
2R-OH + 2Na → 2R-O⁻Na⁺ (Sodium alkoxide) + H₂↑
• Acidic Strength Order: H₂O > 1° Alcohol > 2° Alcohol > 3° Alcohol. Alkyl groups have +I electron-donating effect which increases electron density on oxygen and destabilizes the alkoxide ion.

B. Cleavage of C–O Bond:

• Reaction with HX: R-OH + HX xrightarrow{anh. ZnCl₂} R-X + H₂O (Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°).
• Reaction with PX₃ / PCl₅ / SOCl₂ (Darzens process gives pure R-Cl).

C. Dehydration of Alcohols (Saytzeff's Rule):

Alcohols undergo loss of water upon heating with protic acids (conc. H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄) to yield alkenes:
• Relative ease of dehydration: 3° (20% H₂SO₄, 358 K) > 2° (85% H₃PO₄, 440 K) > 1° (conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K).
• Follows Saytzeff Rule: The more substituted, hyperconjugated alkene is the major product.

D. Oxidation of Alcohols:

• 1° Alcohols: Oxidized to aldehydes using PCC (Pyridinium chlorochromate) or Collins reagent; strong oxidants (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇) oxidize them directly to carboxylic acids.
• 2° Alcohols: Oxidized to ketones with chromic anhydride (CrO₃) or PCC.
• 3° Alcohols: Resist oxidation in neutral/alkaline medium; under drastic acidic conditions undergo dehydration to alkenes followed by cleavage to acids with fewer carbons.

E. Catalytic Dehydrogenation over Heated Cu at 573 K:

• 1° Alcohol xrightarrow{Cu, 573 K} Aldehyde + H₂↑
• 2° Alcohol xrightarrow{Cu, 573 K} Ketone + H₂↑
• 3° Alcohol xrightarrow{Cu, 573 K} Alkene + H₂O (Dehydration occurs instead of dehydrogenation!)

10 Physical Properties of Phenols

Intermolecular vs Intramolecular H-Bonding

• Colorless crystalline solids or liquids; turn pink or reddish brown upon air exposure due to slow oxidation to quinones.
• High boiling points due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
• Steam Volatility of o-Nitrophenol vs p-Nitrophenol (Classic CBSE Question):
    • o-Nitrophenol forms intramolecular hydrogen bonding (chelation) between -OH and adjacent -NO₂ group, preventing association. It is steam volatile with lower boiling point!
    • p-Nitrophenol forms intermolecular hydrogen bonding between adjacent molecules, causing molecular association. It has higher boiling point and is non-steam volatile. They are cleanly separated by steam distillation!

11 Chemical Reactions of Phenols

Acidity & Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

A. Acidity & Reactions of the O–H Bond:

• Reacts with Na: 2C₆H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₆H₅ONa + H₂↑
• Reacts with aq. NaOH: C₆H₅OH + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa + H₂O (Alcohols do NOT react with NaOH!).
• Does NOT react with NaHCO₃: Phenol is weaker than carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) and cannot evolve CO₂.

B. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution:

The -OH group is strongly activating and ortho, para-directing due to resonance (+M effect).
• Bromination:
    • With Bromine water: Yields a dense white precipitate of 2,4,6-Tribromophenol.
    • With Br₂ in non-polar CS₂ or CHCl₃ at 273 K: Yields p-Bromophenol (Major) and o-bromophenol (Minor).
• Nitration:
    • With Dilute HNO₃ at 298 K: Gives mixture of o-Nitrophenol (steam volatile) and p-Nitrophenol.
    • With Concentrated HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄: Gives Picric Acid (2,4,6-Trinitrophenol).

12 Kolbe's Reaction

Synthesis of Salicylic Acid

Phenol is treated with sodium hydroxide to generate sodium phenoxide, which is far more activated than phenol towards electrophilic attack. Carbon dioxide (CO₂, a weak electrophile) is passed through at 400 K and 4–7 atm pressure, followed by acidification to yield Salicylic Acid (2-Hydroxybenzoic acid) as the major product!
C₆H₅ONa + CO₂ xrightarrow{400 K, 4-7 atm} Sodium Salicylate xrightarrow{H⁺} Salicylic Acid
Salicylic acid is acetylated with acetic anhydride to produce Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic acid), an analgesic and antipyretic drug.

When phenol is treated with chloroform (CHCl₃) in the presence of aqueous NaOH at 340 K, followed by alkaline hydrolysis and acidification, an aldehyde group (-CHO) is introduced primarily at the ortho position, forming Salicylaldehyde (2-Hydroxybenzaldehyde)!
C₆H₅OH + CHCl₃ + 3NaOH xrightarrow{340 K} o-(ONa)C₆H₄CHO + 3NaCl + 2H₂O xrightarrow{H⁺} Salicylaldehyde
• Active Electrophile: Neutral Dichlorocarbene (:CCl₂) formed by α-elimination: CHCl₃ + OH⁻ ⇌ :CCl₃⁻ → :CCl₂ + Cl⁻.

16 Acidity of Phenols & Substituent Effects

Resonance of Phenoxide & Substituent Order

Phenol is significantly more acidic than aliphatic alcohols (pKa ~10 vs ~16-18) because:
1. Deprotonation forms the phenoxide ion (C₆H₅O⁻), which is stabilized by resonance delocalization of negative charge over the ortho and para positions of the benzene ring across five canonical structures.
2. In alkoxide ion (R-O⁻), negative charge is localized strictly on oxygen and destabilized by the +I inductive effect of alkyl groups.

📈
Substituent Effects on Phenol Acidity (High-Yield NEET Ladder):
• Electron Withdrawing Groups (EWG: -NO₂, -CN, -X): Disperse negative charge on phenoxide, increasing acidity. The effect is especially pronounced at ortho and para positions due to -M resonance!
• Electron Donating Groups (EDG: -CH₃, -OCH₃, -NH₂): Intensify negative charge, decreasing acidity.
Acidity Hierarchy: Picric acid > 2,4-Dinitrophenol > p-Nitrophenol > o-Nitrophenol > m-Nitrophenol > Phenol > o-Cresol > p-Cresol > m-Cresol > Ethanol

17 Ethers: Classification, Nomenclature & Structure

C–O–C Linkage & 111.7° Bond Angle

Ethers are organic compounds in which an oxygen atom is bonded to two alkyl or aryl groups (R–O–R').
• Classification:
    • Simple / Symmetrical Ethers: Both groups identical (e.g. Diethyl ether C₂H₅OC₂H₅).
    • Mixed / Unsymmetrical Ethers: The two groups differ (e.g. Ethyl methyl ether CH₃OC₂H₅, Anisole C₆H₅OCH₃).
• IUPAC Nomenclature: Named as Alkoxyalkanes. The larger alkyl group forms parent alkane; the smaller alkyl group + oxygen becomes the alkoxy substituent (e.g., CH₃OCH₂CH₃ is Methoxyethane; C₆H₅OCH₃ is Methoxybenzene / Anisole).
• Structure: Oxygen is sp³ hybridized. The C–O–C bond angle is 111.7° (greater than tetrahedral 109.5°) due to repulsive steric hindrance between the two bulky alkyl groups.

18 Preparation of Ethers

Dehydration of Alcohols vs Williamson Synthesis

A. Dehydration of Alcohols:

Ethanol heated with conc. H₂SO₄ at 413 K (140 °C) yields diethyl ether via SN2 bimolecular substitution:
2CH₃CH₂OH xrightarrow{H₂SO₄, 413 K} CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃ (Diethyl ether) + H₂O
• Crucial Temperature Sensitivity: At 443 K (170 °C), dehydration takes the elimination route to yield Ethene (CH₂=CH₂)!
• Suitable only for symmetrical ethers from primary alcohols; 2° and 3° alcohols yield alkenes.

B. Williamson Ether Synthesis:

An alkyl halide is heated with a sodium alkoxide or sodium phenoxide to yield symmetrical or unsymmetrical ethers:
R-X + R'-O⁻Na⁺ xrightarrow{SN2} R-O-R' + NaX
• NEET Critical Condition: Must use a PRIMARY (1°) alkyl halide!
• If a tertiary (3°) alkyl halide is used, alkoxide acts as a strong Bronsted base causing 100% elimination (E2) to give an alkene instead of an ether:
(CH₃)₃C-Br + CH₃O⁻Na⁺ → CH₂=C(CH₃)₂ (Isobutylene) + CH₃OH + NaBr

19 Physical Properties of Ethers

Dipole Moment & Volatility

• Ethers are polar molecules with a net dipole moment (e.g. Diethyl ether μ = 1.18 D) due to the bent C–O–C geometry.
• Boiling Points: Ethers have much lower boiling points than isomeric alcohols because ethers lack intermolecular hydrogen bonding (e.g., Methoxyethane b.p. 281 K vs Propan-1-ol b.p. 370 K). Their boiling points are close to n-alkanes of comparable mass.
• Solubility: Lower ethers (dimethyl ether, diethyl ether) are soluble in water to comparable extents as alcohols because the oxygen atom can accept hydrogen bonds from water molecules.

20 Chemical Reactions of Ethers: Cleavage by Hydrogen Halides

HI Cleavage Rules & Anisole Cleavage

Ethers are relatively inert but undergo cleavage of the C–O bond when heated with concentrated halogen acids (HI or HBr).
• Reactivity of Halogen Acids: HI > HBr > HCl.
• Case 1 (Primary / Secondary Alkyl Groups - SN2 Mechanism):
The iodide ion attacks the smaller (less sterically hindered) alkyl group:
CH₃-O-CH₂CH₃ + HI xrightarrow{373 K} CH₃I (Smaller alkyl iodide) + CH₃CH₂OH (Alcohol)
With excess HI, the alcohol formed is further converted into alkyl iodide: CH₃CH₂OH + HI → CH₃CH₂I + H₂O.

• Case 2 (Ether containing a Tertiary Alkyl Group - SN1 Mechanism):
The reaction proceeds via a stable 3° carbocation intermediate. Hence, the tertiary alkyl group forms the alkyl iodide!
(CH₃)₃C-O-CH₃ + HI → (CH₃)₃C-I (tert-Butyl iodide) + CH₃OH (Methanol)

• Case 3 (Alkyl Aryl Ethers - Anisole Cleavage):
The Ar–O bond has partial double bond character (+M resonance) and is much stronger than the alkyl–O single bond. Hence cleavage occurs exclusively at the alkyl-oxygen bond, yielding Phenol and Alkyl Iodide:
C₆H₅-O-CH₃ + HI xrightarrow{373 K} C₆H₅OH (Phenol) + CH₃I (Methyl iodide)

24 Board-Type "Why" Questions & Summary

High-Priority Exam Reasoning
❓
Why is o-Nitrophenol more steam-volatile than p-Nitrophenol?
In o-nitrophenol, intramolecular hydrogen bonding occurs within the same molecule (chelation). In p-nitrophenol, intermolecular hydrogen bonding binds separate molecules together, requiring more thermal energy to vaporize!
❓
Why does Williamson ether synthesis fail with tertiary alkyl halides?
Sodium alkoxides are strong bases. With bulky tertiary halides, the steric barrier prevents SN2 backside attack, directing the pathway into 100% E2 elimination to yield an alkene!
❓
Why is Phenol acidic, but does not react with NaHCO₃?
Phenoxide ion is stabilized by resonance over the aromatic ring, making phenol acidic (pKa 10). However, phenol is weaker than carbonic acid (pKa 6.35) and cannot displace it from sodium bicarbonate.

1 Introduction to Carbonyl Compounds

Theory & Occurrence

Carbonyl compounds are organic substances containing the carbon-oxygen double bond (>C=O), which constitutes one of the most vital functional groups in organic and biological chemistry.

In aldehydes, the carbonyl group is bonded to at least one hydrogen atom and one alkyl or aryl group (general formula R-CHO, with formaldehyde HCHO having two hydrogens). In ketones, the carbonyl carbon is bonded to two carbon substituents (general formula R-CO-R'), which may be aliphatic or aromatic.

🌿
Natural Occurrence & Fragrances (High-Yield NEET Recall):
  • Vanillin: 4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde, extracted from vanilla beans.
  • Salicylaldehyde: 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde, found in meadowsweet.
  • Cinnamaldehyde: 3-phenylprop-2-enal, extracted from cinnamon bark.
  • Acetophenone: Naturally present in apples, bananas, and apricots.

Carbonyl compounds play a foundational role in living organisms. Carbohydrates (aldoses and ketoses), nucleic acids, microbial pheromones, and steroid hormones (progesterone, cortisone, testosterone) all contain carbonyl groups. Industrially, they are manufactured in millions of tonnes annually as solvents, polymer precursors (e.g. Bakelite), adhesives, and pharmaceuticals.

2 Nomenclature and Structure of Carbonyl Group

IUPAC & Bonding

Under IUPAC nomenclature:
• For open-chain aldehydes, the suffix '-al' replaces the terminal '-e' of the parent alkane. The carbonyl carbon is always numbered as C-1.
• For cyclic aldehydes where the -CHO group is directly attached to a ring, the suffix 'carbaldehyde' is appended to the full name of the cycloalkane (e.g. Cyclohexanecarbaldehyde).
• For ketones, the suffix '-one' replaces the terminal '-e' of the alkane, with the carbonyl position designated by the lowest locant (e.g. Pentan-2-one).

Electronic Structure & Polar Nature of >C=O

The carbonyl carbon is sp² hybridized and forms three coplanar σ-bonds directed towards the corners of an equilateral triangle with bond angles of approximately 120°. The fourth valence electron resides in an unhybridized 2p orbital perpendicular to the σ-plane, overlapping sideways with an oxygen 2p orbital to form a π-bond.

Resonance in Carbonyl Group: >C=O ⟷ >C⁺—O⁻ (Electrophilic Cδ⁺ / Nucleophilic Oδ⁻)
🎯

• The C=O bond length is 1.23 Å, significantly shorter than the C-O single bond (1.43 Å).
• Oxygen has electronegativity 3.5 versus carbon's 2.5. This ΔEN = 1.0 creates a large permanent dipole moment (μ = 2.3 to 2.8 D).
• Consequently, carbonyl compounds are substantially more polar than isomeric ethers (μ ≈ 1.15 D).

4 Physical Properties of Aldehydes and Ketones

Boiling Points & Solubility

1. Physical State & Odour:
• Methanal is a pungent gas at room temperature (b.p. 252 K).
• Ethanal is a volatile liquid boiling at 294 K (21°C).
• Other aldehydes and ketones containing up to 11 carbons are colourless liquids; higher members are solids.
• Lower aldehydes possess sharp pungent odours. As molecular size increases, odour becomes less pungent and more fragrant/fruity (widely used in perfumery).

2. Boiling Point Comparisons (High-Yield Order):
Carbonyl compounds have dipole-dipole attractions because of the polar C=O group. They lack intermolecular hydrogen bonding with themselves (unlike alcohols and carboxylic acids).

🌡️
NEET Standard Boiling Point Hierarchy (Comparable Molecular Weight ≈ 58 - 60 g/mol):
Carboxylic Acids (CH₃COOH: 118°C) > Alcohols (CH₃CH₂CH₂OH: 97°C) > Ketones (CH₃COCH₃: 56°C) > Aldehydes (CH₃CH₂CHO: 49°C) > Ethers (C₂H₅OCH₃: 11°C) > Alkanes (n-Butane: -0.5°C).

3. Solubility in Water:
Lower aldehydes and ketones (Methanal, Ethanal, Propanone) are miscible with water in all proportions because they form intermolecular hydrogen bonds with water molecules through their carbonyl oxygen:
>C=O ··· H—O—H
Solubility drops sharply as alkyl chain length increases beyond 4 carbons, because the non-polar hydrophobic hydrocarbon chain overpowers the polar interaction.

5 Chemical Reactions & Uses of Aldehydes and Ketones

Distinction Tests & Reactions

A. Nucleophilic Addition & Reactivity Order

Aldehydes are generally much more reactive than ketones towards nucleophilic attack due to:
1. Steric effect: Two alkyl groups in ketones crowd the transition state more than one alkyl group in aldehydes.
2. Electronic (+I) effect: Two electron-releasing alkyl groups in ketones reduce the partial positive charge (δ⁺) on carbonyl carbon more effectively.

Relative Reactivity: HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃CH₂CHO > CH₃COCH₃ > C₆H₅CHO > C₆H₅COCH₃

B. NEET Distinction Tests

1. Tollens' Test (Silver Mirror Test):
R-CHO + 2 [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3 OH⁻ ⟶ R-COO⁻ + 2 Ag↓ (Silver Mirror) + 4 NH₃ + 2 H₂O
(Positive for ALL aldehydes; negative for ketones)
2. Fehling's Test:
R-CHO + 2 Cu²⁺ + 5 OH⁻ ⟶ R-COO⁻ + Cu₂O↓ (Red-Brown Ppt) + 3 H₂O
(Positive for ALIPHATIC aldehydes only! Aromatic aldehydes like Benzaldehyde DO NOT react!)
3. Iodoform Reaction (Haloform Test):
CH₃-CO-R + 3 I₂ + 4 NaOH ⟶ R-COONa + CHI₃↓ (Yellow Ppt, m.p. 119°C) + 3 NaI + 3 H₂O
(Positive for all methyl ketones and acetaldehyde CH₃CHO, plus CH₃-CH(OH)- alcohols)

C. Aldol Condensation vs Cannizzaro Reaction

• Aldol Condensation: Aldehydes/ketones having at least one α-hydrogen heated with dilute alkali (dil. NaOH) form β-hydroxyaldehydes (aldol), which upon heating lose H₂O to form α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds (e.g. But-2-enal from ethanal).
• Cannizzaro Reaction: Aldehydes having no α-hydrogen (HCHO, C₆H₅CHO, (CH₃)₃C-CHO) undergo disproportionation (self oxidation-reduction) with concentrated alkali (50% NaOH) to give one molecule of alcohol and one molecule of carboxylic acid salt.

D. Prominent Industrial & Medical Uses

• Formalin: 40% aqueous solution of formaldehyde, widely used to preserve biological specimens and embalm tissue.
• Bakelite: Formed by condensation polymerization of phenol and formaldehyde.
• Acetone: Foremost laboratory and industrial solvent for cellulose acetate, nitrocellulose, lacquers, and varnishes.
• Benzaldehyde: Known as oil of bitter almonds, used in perfumery, cosmetics, and dye synthesis.

6 Carboxylic Acids: Structure and Nomenclature

Resonance & Bonding

Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl functional group (-COOH), comprised of a carbonyl group (>C=O) attached directly to a hydroxyl group (-OH).

Resonance of the Carboxyl Group

Unlike simple carbonyl compounds, the lone pair of electrons on the hydroxyl oxygen is delocalized into the carbonyl π-system:

R-C(=O)-OH ⟷ R-C(-O⁻)=O⁺H

Consequence for Reactivity: This resonance delocalization reduces the partial positive charge on the carbonyl carbon. Therefore, the carbonyl carbon in carboxylic acids is much less electrophilic than in aldehydes and ketones, and carboxylic acids do not undergo nucleophilic addition reactions characteristic of aldehydes and ketones.

8 Physical Properties of Carboxylic Acids

Dimerization & B.P.

1. Unusually High Boiling Points:
Carboxylic acids boil at significantly higher temperatures than alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, or ethers of comparable molecular mass. For example, ethanoic acid (M = 60) boils at 118°C, while propan-1-ol (M = 60) boils at 97°C.

🔗
Explanation of Stable Cyclic Dimerization:
In carboxylic acids, the hydroxyl group is strongly polarized by the adjacent carbonyl oxygen. Two molecules of carboxylic acid form an exceptionally stable 8-membered cyclic dimer held by two intermolecular hydrogen bonds.
• These dimers persist even in the vapour phase and in non-polar solvents like benzene.
• In benzene, the measured molecular mass of acetic acid is 120 g/mol (double the formula mass of 60 g/mol!).

2. Water Solubility:
Aliphatic carboxylic acids having up to 4 carbon atoms (methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic, butanoic) are miscible with water in all proportions because of strong hydrogen bonding with water molecules.
Solubility diminishes rapidly for higher carboxylic acids due to the increasing size of the hydrophobic hydrocarbon portion. Benzoic acid is practically insoluble in cold water.

9 Chemical Properties & Acidity of Carboxylic Acids

Acidity, HVZ & Decarboxylation

A. Acidity and Carboxylate Ion Resonance

Carboxylic acids are the most acidic simple organic compounds. They react with active metals (Na, K) to liberate H₂, with strong bases (NaOH) to form salts, and with weak bases like NaHCO₃ to generate brisk effervescence of CO₂ gas:

R-COOH + NaHCO₃ ⟶ R-COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑ (Brisk Effervescence)

📌 Crucial NEET Distinction: Phenols DO NOT liberate CO₂ from aqueous NaHCO₃ because phenols are weaker acids (pKa ≈ 10) than carbonic acid (pKa ≈ 6.4). Carboxylic acids (pKa ≈ 4-5) are stronger than carbonic acid.

Why is Carboxylate Ion More Stable than Phenoxide Ion?

In carboxylate ion (RCOO⁻), the negative charge is delocalized over two identical highly electronegative oxygen atoms (bond length 1.27 Å, bond order 1.5). In phenoxide ion, the negative charge is delocalized onto less electronegative carbon atoms of the benzene ring. Hence, carboxylate is far more stable, making carboxylic acids much stronger acids than phenols!

⚖️
Effect of Substituents on Acidity (Decreasing Order of -I / -M Effect):
-CF₃ > -NO₂ > -CN > -F > -Cl > -Br > -I > -Ph
• Electron-withdrawing groups disperse negative charge ⟶ stabilize RCOO⁻ ⟶ Increase acidity (lower pKa).
• Electron-donating (+I) alkyl groups intensify negative charge ⟶ Decrease acidity.
CF₃COOH > CCl₃COOH > CHCl₂COOH > NO₂CH₂COOH > FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > HCOOH > C₆H₅COOH > CH₃COOH

B. Reactions Involving C-OH Cleavage

1. Anhydride Formation: 2 CH₃COOH ⟶[P₂O₅, Δ] (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O
2. Esterification (Fischer): R-COOH + R'-OH ⇌[H₂SO₄] R-COOR' + H₂O
3. Acid Chlorides: R-COOH + SOCl₂ ⟶ R-COCl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑ (SOCl₂ preferred as byproducts are gases!)
4. With Ammonia: CH₃COOH + NH₃ ⇌ CH₃COONH₄ ⟶[Δ, -H₂O] CH₃CONH₂ (Acetamide)

R-CH₂-COOH + Br₂ ⟶[Red P / H₂O] R-CH(Br)-COOH (α-Bromocarboxylic acid)

-COOH is meta-directing and deactivating. Nitration gives m-nitrobenzoic acid.

1 Classification of Amines

NCERT Sec 9.1 & 1°, 2°, 3° Systems

Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia (NH₃) obtained by replacing one, two, or all three hydrogen atoms with alkyl or aryl groups.
• Primary (1°) Amines: One hydrogen replaced by R/Ar (functional group: -NH₂, amino group). E.g., Methylamine (CH₃NH₂), Aniline (C₆H₅NH₂).
• Secondary (2°) Amines: Two hydrogens replaced (functional group: >NH, imino group). E.g., Dimethylamine ((CH₃)₂NH).
• Tertiary (3°) Amines: All three hydrogens replaced (functional group: >N-, tertiary nitrogen). E.g., Trimethylamine ((CH₃)₃N).
• Aliphatic vs Aromatic: In aliphatic amines, nitrogen is bonded to alkyl groups; in aromatic amines (arylamines), nitrogen is bonded directly to one or more aryl rings.

2 Structure & Geometry of Amines

sp³ Nitrogen & Pyramidal Shape

Nitrogen in amines is sp³ hybridized with tetrahedral electron geometry and a trigonal pyramidal shape. Three of the sp³ orbitals overlap with orbitals of carbon or hydrogen, while the fourth orbital houses an unshared lone pair of electrons.
• Bond Angle: The C–N–C bond angle in trimethylamine is 108°, slightly less than regular tetrahedral angle (109.5°) due to lone pair–bond pair repulsions.
• Pyramidal Inversion (Umbrella Inversion): The unshared pair flips rapidly through the nitrogen nucleus from one side to the other, making simple chiral trivalent amines racemic mixtures at room temperature.

3 Nomenclature of Amines

Alkylamine vs Alkanamine System

• Common System: Named by adding suffix '-amine' to the alkyl group (e.g. Methylamine, Ethylmethylamine).
• IUPAC System: Suffix '-amine' replaces terminal '-e' of parent alkane (e.g. Methanamine, Ethanamine). For secondary and tertiary amines, the locant 'N-' designates substituents attached to nitrogen (e.g. N-Methylethanamine, N,N-Dimethylmethanamine).
• Aromatic Amines: Simplest arylamine is Benzenamine, commonly called Aniline (accepted by IUPAC).

4 Preparation: Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides

Hoffmann's Ammonolysis

An alkyl or benzyl halide heated with ethanolic ammonia in a sealed tube at 373 K undergoes nucleophilic substitution:
NH₃ (Nu) + R-X → R-NH₃⁺X⁻ xrightarrow{NH₃} R-NH₂ (1° Amine) + NH₄⁺X⁻
• Limitation: The primary amine formed is itself a good nucleophile and reacts further with remaining alkyl halide to yield a mixture of secondary amine, tertiary amine, and quaternary ammonium salt (R₄N⁺X⁻)!
• Control: If large excess of ammonia (NH₃) is used, the primary amine is obtained as the predominant major product!

5 Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis

Pure 1° Aliphatic Amines

Phthalimide is treated with ethanolic KOH to form potassium phthalimide. Heating with an alkyl halide (R-X) produces N-alkylphthalimide via SN2 substitution, which upon alkaline hydrolysis yields a pure primary aliphatic amine:
Phthalimide xrightarrow{KOH} Potassium Phthalimide xrightarrow{R-X (1°)} N-Alkylphthalimide xrightarrow{aq. NaOH} R-NH₂ + Sodium Phthalate
• CRITICAL BOARD LIMITATION: Aromatic primary amines (like aniline) CANNOT be prepared by Gabriel synthesis because aryl halides (Ar-X) do not undergo nucleophilic substitution with potassium phthalimide anion under normal conditions!

6 Hofmann Bromamide Degradation Reaction

Amide to 1° Amine (1 Less Carbon)

An acid amide is treated with bromine in an aqueous or ethanolic solution of sodium hydroxide (Br₂ + 4NaOH). The reaction involves migration of an alkyl or aryl group from carbonyl carbon to nitrogen, accompanied by loss of the carbonyl carbon as carbonate:
R-CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → R-NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O
• High-Yield Key Point: The amine product contains ONE CARBON ATOM LESS than the starting amide (descent of carbon series). E.g., Ethanamide (CH₃CONH₂) gives Methanamine (CH₃NH₂); Benzamide (C₆H₅CONH₂) gives Aniline (C₆H₅NH₂).

7 Preparation of Aromatic Amines (Reduction of Nitrobenzene)

Fe/HCl vs Sn/HCl

Nitro compounds are reduced to primary aromatic amines by catalytic hydrogenation with H₂/Ni, or by treatment with metals in acidic medium:
C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] xrightarrow{Fe / HCl text{ or } Sn / HCl} C₆H₅NH₂ (Aniline) + 2H₂O
• Why is Fe/HCl preferred over Sn/HCl? (CBSE Question):
Iron scrap and hydrochloric acid (Fe + HCl) is favored commercially because FeCl₂ produced is hydrolyzed by water to regenerate HCl! Hence, only a catalytic scrap amount of hydrochloric acid is required to initiate the reaction.

8 Physical Properties of Amines

Boiling Points & Solubility

• Physical State & Smell: Lower aliphatic amines are gases with characteristic fishy odors; primary amines with 3 or more carbons are liquids. Aniline is colorless oily liquid but turns brown on storage due to atmospheric oxidation.
• Boiling Points: Order among isomeric amines: Primary (1°) > Secondary (2°) > Tertiary (3°). Primary amines have two N–H hydrogens for extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding; secondary amines have one; tertiary amines have NO N–H bonds and cannot associate with each other via H-bonding.
• Solubility: Lower aliphatic amines form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and are freely soluble. Solubility decreases with increasing molecular mass. Aniline is practically insoluble in water.

9 & 10 Basic Character & Strength of Amines

The Master Basicity Ladder

Amines are basic due to the availability of an unshared lone pair of electrons on nitrogen to donate to a proton (Lewis base / Bronsted-Lowry base).
• Alkylamines vs Ammonia: Alkylamines are stronger bases than ammonia in the gas phase because electron-donating alkyl groups (+I effect) push electron density onto nitrogen and disperse the positive charge of the substituted ammonium cation.

⚖️
AQUEOUS PHASE BASICITY ORDER (Top NEET Question): In aqueous medium, basic strength depends on three competing factors: Inductive effect (+I), Hydration / Solvation energy, and Steric hindrance!
• For Methyl-substituted series: (CH₃)₂NH (2°) > CH₃NH₂ (1°) > (CH₃)₃N (3°) > NH₃ (Rule: 213)
• For Ethyl-substituted series: (C₂H₅)₂NH (2°) > (C₂H₅)₃N (3°) > C₂H₅NH₂ (1°) > NH₃ (Rule: 231)
• In Gas Phase (pure +I effect): 3° Amine > 2° Amine > 1° Amine > NH₃

Why is Aniline far less basic than Ammonia? (pKb = 9.38 vs 4.75): In aniline, the lone pair on nitrogen is delocalized into the benzene π-system across five resonance contributors. Consequently, electron density on nitrogen is reduced. Furthermore, the anilinium ion (C₆H₅NH₃⁺) has only two resonance structures, making protonation unfavorable!

11 - 13 Acylation, Carbylamine & Hinsberg's Test

A. Acylation of Amines:

Primary and secondary amines react with acid chlorides or anhydrides in presence of pyridine base to form substituted amides (N-acetyl derivatives):
CH₃CH₂NH₂ + CH₃COCl xrightarrow{text{pyridine}} CH₃CH₂NH-COCH₃ (N-Ethylethanamide) + HCl

R-NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3KOH (alc.) xrightarrow{Δ} R-NC (Carbylamine, extremely foul odor) + 3KCl + 3H₂O

• Tertiary Amine: Does NOT react with benzenesulphonyl chloride; remains insoluble in alkali, dissolves in acid.

1 & 2 Carbohydrates & Structure of Glucose

NCERT Sec 10.1 & Open-Chain Reactions

Carbohydrates are defined as optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones, or substances which produce such units on hydrolysis.
• Classification: Monosaccharides (cannot be hydrolyzed further, e.g. Glucose, Fructose), Oligosaccharides (yield 2 to 10 monosaccharides, e.g. Disaccharides Sucrose, Maltose, Lactose), and Polysaccharides (yield hundreds of monosaccharide units, e.g. Starch, Cellulose, Glycogen).

Chemical Evidence for Open-Chain Structure of D-(+)-Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆):

2. Carbonyl Group (>C=O): Reacts with hydroxylamine (NH₂OH) to form an oxime and adds HCN to form a cyanohydrin.
3. Aldehyde Group (-CHO): Mild oxidation with bromine water yields 6-carbon carboxylic acid Gluconic acid (HOCH₂-(CHOH)₄-COOH).
4. Five Hydroxyl Groups (-OH): Acetylation with acetic anhydride forms Glucose pentaacetate, confirming 5 -OH groups attached to five different carbons!
5. Primary Alcohol Group (-CH₂OH): Oxidation with strong nitric acid (HNO₃) yields a dicarboxylic acid called Saccharic acid (HOOC-(CHOH)₄-COOH).

3 & 4 Cyclic Structure of Glucose & Mutarotation

Haworth Pyranose & Anomers

Open-chain glucose fails to form 2,4-DNP adduct, does not form sodium bisulphite adduct, and glucose pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine. This proves that the -CHO group is not completely free.
• Intramolecular Hemiacetal: The -OH group at C-5 adds across the C-1 carbonyl group to form a six-membered pyranose ring.
• Anomeric Carbon (C-1): Becomes chiral, yielding two stereoisomers called anomers:
    • α-D-Glucopyranose: C-1 -OH points DOWNWARDS (trans to C-5 -CH₂OH); [α]D = +112.2°.
    • β-D-Glucopyranose: C-1 -OH points UPWARDS (cis to C-5 -CH₂OH); [α]D = +18.7°.
• Mutarotation: When either pure α or β form is dissolved in water, the optical rotation spontaneously changes over time until reaching an equilibrium value of +52.7° (composed of ~36% α + 64% β + 0.02% open chain).

5 & 6 Disaccharides & Reducing vs Non-Reducing Sugars

Sucrose, Maltose & Inversion of Sugar

Disaccharides consist of two monosaccharide units joined by a glycosidic linkage (C–O–C oxide bond formed by condensation):

1. Sucrose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ - Cane Sugar):
Composed of α-D-glucopyranose and β-D-fructofuranose joined by an α(1 → 2) glycosidic linkage.
• Non-reducing Sugar: Both reducing anomeric carbons (C-1 of glucose and C-2 of fructose) are engaged in glycosidic bond formation; no free hemiacetal or hemiketal group is present!
• Invert Sugar: Sucrose is dextrorotatory ([α]D = +66.5°). On hydrolysis by acid or invertase enzyme, it produces an equimolar mixture of D-(+)-glucose (+52.5°) and D-(-)-fructose (-92.4°). Because laevorotation of fructose is greater than dextrorotation of glucose, the overall optical rotation changes from dextro (+) to laevo (-)! This hydrolyzed mixture is termed Invert Sugar.

2. Maltose: Two α-D-glucose units joined by α(1 → 4) linkage. Reducing sugar because C-1 of the second glucose unit has a free hemiacetal.

3. Lactose (Milk Sugar): β-D-galactose and β-D-glucose joined by β(1 → 4) linkage. Reducing sugar.

7 & 8 Polysaccharides: Starch, Cellulose & Glycogen

Amylose vs Amylopectin

Polysaccharides are high-molecular-weight polymers of monosaccharides linked by glycosidic bonds.

1. Starch (Main Storage Polysaccharide of Plants):
Composed of two structural fractions:
• Amylose (15–20%): Water-soluble, linear unbranched polymer of α-D-glucose held by α(1 → 4) glycosidic linkages. Forms helical coil giving characteristic blue color with iodine (I₂).
• Amylopectin (80–85%): Water-insoluble, branched polymer. Main chains held by α(1 → 4) linkages; branching occurs via α(1 → 6) glycosidic linkages every 20–25 glucose residues! Gives reddish-brown color with I₂.

2. Cellulose:
Predominant structural component of plant cell walls. Linear unbranched polymer of β-D-glucose held by β(1 → 4) glycosidic linkages. Chains pack closely in parallel sheets stabilized by extensive hydrogen bonding. Humans cannot digest cellulose because our digestive tract lacks the enzyme cellulase.

3. Glycogen (Animal Starch):
Carbohydrate stored in animal liver and muscles. Structurally similar to amylopectin, but more highly branched!

9 - 12 Amino Acids, Zwitterion & Peptide Bond

Dipolar Ion & Condensation

Proteins are polymers of α-amino acids (compounds containing an amino group -NH₂ and a carboxyl group -COOH attached to the same α-carbon: R-CH(NH₂)-COOH).
• Essential Amino Acids (10 Must be ingested in diet): Valine, Leucine, Isoleucine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan, Threonine, Histidine, Arginine.
• Optical Inactivity of Glycine: All natural amino acids are optically active (chiral) EXCEPT Glycine (H₂N-CH₂-COOH) which has two identical hydrogen atoms on the α-carbon!

⚡
Zwitterion & Isoelectric Point (pI): In aqueous solution, the acidic -COOH group transfers a proton to the basic -NH₂ group to form a dipolar ion called a Zwitterion:
R-CH(NH₂)-COOH ⇌ R-CH(NH₃⁺)-COO⁻ (Zwitterion, net charge = 0)
• Zwitterions are amphoteric (react with both acids and bases).
• Isoelectric Point (pI): The specific pH at which the dipolar amino acid carries no net electrical charge and does not migrate towards either electrode in an electric field.

• The Peptide Bond (-CO-NH-): A covalent amide linkage formed by condensation between the -COOH group of one amino acid and the -NH₂ group of another with loss of water:
H₂N-CH(R₁)-COOH + H₂N-CH(R₂)-COOH → H₂N-CH(R₁)-CO-NH-CH(R₂)-COOH + H₂O
Possesses partial double bond character due to resonance, making the peptide unit rigid and planar.

13 - 15 Structure of Proteins & Denaturation

Primary to Quaternary & Loss of Conformation

Proteins exhibit four levels of structural hierarchy:
• Primary Structure: Specific sequence of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. A change in sequence destroys biological function (e.g., Sickle-cell anaemia: 6th position of β-globin changed from Glutamic acid to Valine).
• Secondary Structure: Regular folding of polypeptide chain stabilized by hydrogen bonds:
    • α-Helix: Right-handed helix stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding between -NH of residue n and >C=O of residue n+4 (e.g. Keratin in hair/nails).
    • β-Pleated Sheet: Extended polypeptide chains lying side-by-side held by intermolecular hydrogen bonds (e.g. Silk fibroin).
• Tertiary Structure: Overall 3D folding of polypeptide into fibrous or globular shapes stabilized by disulfide bonds (-S-S-), ionic electrostatic bonds, H-bonds, and hydrophobic interactions.
• Quaternary Structure: Spatial arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits (e.g. Hemoglobin: 4 subunits, 2α + 2β).

🍳
Denaturation of Proteins (Guaranteed CBSE Question): When a protein is subjected to heat, changes in pH, or chemical agents, hydrogen bonds and tertiary folding are disrupted.
• Primary structure remains completely intact (peptide bonds are NOT broken).
• Secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed, causing loss of biological activity.
Examples: Coagulation of egg white on boiling; curdling of milk by bacteria.

1-2 Electric Current & Drift Velocity in Conductors

Class 12 Physics • Ch. 3

Electric Current: Defined as the rate of flow of electric charge across any cross-section of a conductor.
• Average Current: I = Q / t
• Instantaneous Current: I = dQ / dt
• SI Unit: Ampere (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s. Conventional current direction is taken along the direction of positive charge flow, which is opposite to the direction of electron flow.

Microscopic Model & Drift Velocity:

In a metallic conductor, free electrons undergo constant random thermal collisions with positive metal ions at speeds of ~10⁵–10⁶ m/s, resulting in zero net macroscopic flow. In the presence of an applied electric field E, electrons experience an electrostatic force F = -eE and acquire a steady average drift velocity:

vd = (e · E · τ) / m

where e = electronic charge (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C), E = electric field, τ = mean relaxation time (average time between two successive collisions), and m = electron mass (9.1×10⁻³¹ kg).

Relation Between Current and Drift Velocity:

I = n · e · A · vd

where n is the free electron number density (carriers/m³), A is the conductor cross-sectional area, and Current Density j = I / A = n · e · vd (a vector quantity pointing along E).

⚡
Why is drift velocity very small (~10⁻⁴ m/s) yet a bulb lights up instantly? Electrons suffer frequent collisions with lattice ions (τ ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ s), preventing unbounded acceleration. However, the electric field propagates through the wire at nearly the speed of light (~3×10⁸ m/s), initiating coordinated electron drift everywhere in the circuit almost instantaneously!

3 Mobility of Charge Carriers

Carrier Dynamics

Mobility (μ) is defined as the magnitude of drift velocity acquired per unit electric field:

μ = vd / E = (e · τ) / m

• SI Unit: m² · V⁻¹ · s⁻¹ (or m² / (V · s)).
• Mobility is always defined as a positive quantity regardless of charge carrier sign (for electrons, μe = eτe / me; for holes, μh = eτh / mh).
• Since electron mass is much smaller than effective hole mass, electron mobility is significantly higher than hole mobility (μe > μh).

4-7 Ohm's Law, Resistance, Resistivity & Conductivity

Constitutive Laws

Ohm's Law: The potential difference V across the ends of a conductor is directly proportional to the current I flowing through it, provided physical conditions (temperature, pressure, mechanical strain) remain constant:

V = I · R   ⇒   Vector Form: j = σ · E

Resistance of a Conductor:

R = ρ · (l / A) = (m / (n · e² · τ)) · (l / A)

where l is length, A is cross-sectional area, and ρ is the electrical resistivity of the material.
• Resistivity (ρ): ρ = (R · A) / l = m / (n · e² · τ). Material property; independent of dimensions; depends only on material nature and temperature. SI unit: Ω • m.
• Conductivity (σ): Reciprocal of resistivity: σ = 1 / ρ = (n · e² · τ) / m. SI unit: Siemens per metre (S • m⁻¹ or Ω⁻¹ • m⁻¹).

💡
Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic Conductors:
• Ohmic Conductors: Obey Ohm's law with linear V-I characteristics passing through the origin (e.g., metals at constant temperature, dilute electrolytes).
• Non-Ohmic Conductors: Do not obey Ohm's law; V-I relation is non-linear, non-unique, or asymmetric upon reversing V (e.g., p-n junction diode, LED, vacuum tube, thyristor, GaAs).

8 Temperature Dependence of Resistance

Thermal Coefficients

The resistance of a metallic conductor varies with temperature according to:

R_T = R₀ [1 + α (T - T₀)]   ⇒   ρ_T = ρ₀ [1 + α (T - T₀)]

where α is the temperature coefficient of resistance (SI unit: K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹):
• Metals: Positive α (α > 0). As temperature increases, lattice ions vibrate more vigorously, reducing mean relaxation time τ (more collisions) ⟶ Resistance increases.
• Semiconductors & Insulators: Negative α (α < 0). Increasing temperature breaks covalent bonds, causing carrier density n to increase exponentially, dominating over collision effects ⟶ Resistance decreases.
• Standard Alloys (Constantan, Manganin, Nichrome): Extremely small positive α and high resistivity; resistance changes negligibly with temperature, making them ideal for standard resistors, resistance boxes, and meter bridges!

9 Combination of Resistors (Series & Parallel)

Circuit Analysis
Resistors in Series

R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...
• Same current through each resistor.
• Potential divides: V = V₁ + V₂ + ...
• Equivalent resistance is greater than the largest individual resistance.

Resistors in Parallel

1 / R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...
• Same voltage across each resistor.
• Current divides: I = I₁ + I₂ + ...
• Equivalent resistance is smaller than the smallest individual resistance.

10-13 Cells, EMF, Internal Resistance & Combinations

Sources of EMF

• Electromotive Force (EMF, E): Maximum potential difference between cell terminals in an open circuit (no current drawn): E = W / q (Unit: Volt).
• Internal Resistance (r): Opposition to current flow offered by the electrolyte and electrodes inside the cell.
• Terminal Potential Difference (V): Potential difference across terminals when current I flows through external load R:

V = E - I · r   and   I = E / (R + r)

📌 When cell is charging: Current is pushed into the positive terminal, so V = E + I · r (Terminal voltage exceeds EMF!).

Combinations of n Identical Cells:

• Cells in Series: E_eq = n · E, r_eq = n · r ⟶ I = (n · E) / (R + n · r). Most advantageous when external resistance is very large (R ≫ nr).
• Cells in Parallel: E_eq = E, r_eq = r / n ⟶ I = E / (R + r/n). Most advantageous when external resistance is very small (R ≪ r).
• Mixed Combination: m rows of n cells: Maximum current occurs when R = (n · r) / m (Internal resistance equals external load resistance).

14-15 Kirchhoff's Circuit Rules & Sign Conventions

Major Board Derivation

1. Kirchhoff's First Rule (Junction Rule):
At any junction, the algebraic sum of currents entering the junction equals the sum leaving it:

Σ I_in = Σ I_out   (Conservation of Electric Charge)

2. Kirchhoff's Second Rule (Loop Rule):
The algebraic sum of changes in potential around any closed mesh/loop is zero:

Σ ΔV = 0   (Conservation of Energy)
⚖️
CBSE Loop Rule Sign Conventions:
• Traversing a resistor in direction of current: - I · R (potential drop).
• Traversing a resistor opposite to current: + I · R (potential rise).
• Traversing a cell from negative to positive terminal: + E (potential gain).
• Traversing a cell from positive to negative terminal: - E (potential loss).

16-17 Wheatstone Bridge & Meter Bridge

Practical Measurement

Balanced Wheatstone Bridge: Four resistors P, Q, R, S arranged in a quadrilateral bridge. When no current flows through the central galvanometer (I_g = 0):

P / Q = R / S   (Bridge Balance Condition)

Meter Bridge (Practical Application): Consists of a 1-metre (100 cm) uniform manganin wire. A known resistance R is placed in the left gap and an unknown resistance S in the right gap. If the null balance point is found at distance l cm from the zero end:

R / S = l / (100 - l)   ⇒   S = R · (100 - l) / l
🎯
Why is balance point preferably near the middle (40–60 cm)? The bridge sensitivity is maximum when all four arms have nearly equal resistance, which minimizes fractional error in measuring length Δl / l and end resistance corrections!

18-20 Electrical Energy, Power & Joule Heating

Thermal Effects

• Electrical Work & Energy: W = V · I · t = I² · R · t = (V² / R) · t (Unit: Joule).
• Electrical Power: P = V · I = I² · R = V² / R (Unit: Watt, where 1 W = 1 J/s).
• Commercial Unit: Kilowatt-hour (kWh) or Board of Trade unit:

1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

• Joule's Law of Heating: Heat produced H = I² · R · t. Applications: Electric heater, iron, toaster, electric fuse (alloy of lead and tin with low melting point and high resistance).

As temperature rises, amplitude of lattice ion vibrations increases. Free electrons collide more frequently, reducing relaxation time τ. Since ρ = m / (ne²τ), decreased τ results in increased resistivity.

Series connection ensures the entire branch current passes through the ammeter. Parallel connection ensures the voltmeter experiences the exact potential difference across the desired two nodes.

1-2 Magnetic Field & Lorentz Force on Moving Charges

Class 12 Physics • Ch. 4

Magnetic Field (B): Space surrounding a magnet or current-carrying conductor in which magnetic forces are experienced. SI unit: Tesla (T), where 1 T = 1 N / (A · m) = 10⁴ Gauss.
• Magnetic Field Lines: Closed continuous loops (emerge from North, enter South outside; continue South to North inside). Tangent gives B direction. Field lines never intersect (otherwise two directions would exist at a single point, which is impossible!).

Magnetic Lorentz Force:

F = q (v × B)   ⇒   F = q · v · B · sin θ

• Special Cases:
  • Charge at rest (v = 0) ⟶ F = 0 (A stationary charge experiences zero magnetic force!).
  • Motion parallel/antiparallel (θ = 0° or 180°) ⟶ F = 0.
  • Motion perpendicular (θ = 90°) ⟶ Maximum force F_max = q · v · B.
• Direction: Given by Right-Hand Thumb Rule or Fleming's Left-Hand Rule. For negative charges (electrons), the force direction is exactly reversed!

3 Motion of a Charged Particle in Uniform B-Field

Circular & Helical Trajectories

Case A: Velocity Perpendicular to B (θ = 90°):
The magnetic force acts as the centripetal force: q · v · B = (m · v²) / r.

r = (m · v) / (q · B)  |  T = (2π · m) / (q · B)  |  f = (q · B) / (2π · m)

• CRITICAL NCERT INSIGHT: Time period T and frequency f are completely independent of the particle's speed v and orbit radius r! Faster particles travel along larger circles, completing each revolution in the exact same time.

Case B: Velocity at Oblique Angle (Helical Path):
• Parallel component v_∥ = v cos θ is unaffected (causes uniform translation along B).
• Perpendicular component v_⊥ = v sin θ causes circular motion with radius r = (m · v_⊥) / (qB).
• Resultant path is a Helix with pitch p = v_∥ · T = (v cos θ) · (2πm / qB).

4 The Cyclotron Particle Accelerator

Classic Board Derivation

Principle: A charged particle is repeatedly accelerated across an alternating electric field while a perpendicular magnetic field bends it into expanding semicircular paths inside two hollow D-shaped metal chambers (dees).
• Cyclotron Resonance Frequency: f_c = (q · B) / (2π · m).
• Maximum Kinetic Energy: When particle exits at dee radius R:

K_max = ½ m v_max² = (q² · B² · R²) / (2m)
⚠️
Limitations of Cyclotron:
1. Cannot accelerate neutral particles (neutrons) as they experience zero electric/magnetic force.
2. Cannot accelerate electrons effectively because their tiny mass causes relativistic mass increase m = m₀ / √(1 - v²/c²) at relatively low energies, falling out of resonance with the oscillator!

5-7 Force on Conductor & Between Parallel Wires

B-Field Forces

Force on Current-Carrying Conductor: F = I (L × B) ⟶ F = B · I · L · sin θ. Direction given by Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.

Force Between Two Long Parallel Conductors:

F / L = (μ₀ · I₁ · I₂) / (2π · d)

• Currents in same direction: ATTRACT each other.
• Currents in opposite direction: REPEL each other.
• Definition of Ampere: 1 Ampere is that steady current which, when maintained in two straight parallel conductors of infinite length placed 1 metre apart in vacuum, produces between them a force of 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre of length.

8-9 Torque on Current Loop & Magnetic Dipole Moment

Dipole Interactions

A planar current loop placed in a uniform magnetic field experiences zero net force but a net deflecting torque:

τ = m × B   ⇒   τ = N · I · A · B · sin θ

where m = N · I · A is the magnetic dipole moment of the loop (SI unit: A · m² or J / T).
• Maximum torque at θ = 90° (loop plane parallel to B).
• Zero torque at θ = 0° or 180° (loop plane perpendicular to B).

10-14 Moving Coil Galvanometer & Conversions

Core Board Instrument

Principle: A current-carrying coil placed in a magnetic field experiences a deflecting torque τ = N · I · A · B. Under a radial magnetic field (produced by concave pole pieces and a soft iron core), the coil plane is always parallel to field lines (sin θ = 1).
At equilibrium, deflecting torque = restoring torque: N · I · A · B = k · θ.

θ = (N · A · B / k) · I   ⇒   Deflection θ ∝ Current I

• Current Sensitivity: θ / I = (N · A · B) / k.
• Voltage Sensitivity: θ / V = (N · A · B) / (k · G).
• Conversion into Ammeter: Connect a small shunt S = (I_g · G) / (I - I_g) in parallel.
• Conversion into Voltmeter: Connect high resistance R = (V / I_g) - G in series.

15-18 Biot–Savart Law & Magnetic Fields of Currents

Field Derivations

Biot–Savart Law: Magnetic field dB due to an infinitesimal current element I · dl:

dB = (μ₀ / 4π) · (I · dl · sin θ) / r²   where   μ₀ / 4π = 10⁻⁷ T·m/A

• Long Straight Wire: B = (μ₀ · I) / (2π · r) (Direction given by Right-Hand Thumb Rule).
• Centre of Circular Loop (N turns): B = (μ₀ · N · I) / (2R).
• Axis of Circular Loop at distance x:

B_axis = (μ₀ · N · I · R²) / [2 (R² + x²)^(3/2)]

19-21 Ampere's Circuital Law, Solenoid & Toroid

Circuital Theorems

Ampere's Circuital Law: The line integral of magnetic field B around any closed loop equals μ₀ times the total current enclosed by the loop:

∮ B · dl = μ₀ · I_enclosed

• Long Ideal Solenoid: B = μ₀ · n · I (where n = N / L is turns per unit length). Field inside is uniform and parallel to the axis; field outside is negligibly weak (~0).
• Toroid (Endless Solenoid): B = (μ₀ · N · I) / (2π · r) inside the core; zero in open space inside and outside the toroid.

Q: Why does a magnetic field not change the kinetic energy of a charged particle?

Magnetic force F = q(v × B) is perpendicular to velocity v at every instant. Power delivered P = F · v = 0. By work-energy theorem, work done is zero, so speed and KE remain constant.

In a radial field, field lines always lie in the plane of the coil (θ = 90°), making deflecting torque maximum and constant for a given current, which ensures a linear scale (θ ∝ I).

21-27 Magnetic Hysteresis & Technical Magnets

Materials Engineering

Hysteresis: The lagging of magnetisation B behind the magnetising field H in ferromagnetic materials.
• Retentivity (Remanence): Value of B remaining when H is reduced to zero.
• Coercivity: Reverse magnetising field (-H) required to reduce residual B to zero.
• Hysteresis Loss: Energy dissipated as heat per cycle of magnetisation equals the area of the B-H loop!

Permanent Magnets (Steel, Alnico)

• High Retentivity: Stays strongly magnetised.
• High Coercivity: Resistant to stray demagnetisation.
• Broad B-H loop; hard magnetic material.

Electromagnets & Cores (Soft Iron)

• High Initial Permeability: Produces intense B for small I.
• Low Coercivity & Retentivity: Demagnetises readily.
• Narrow B-H loop (minimal hysteresis heat loss in transformers).

4 Lenz's Law & Conservation of Energy

Foundational Board Topic

Statement: The polarity of induced EMF is such that it tends to produce a current which opposes the change in magnetic flux that produces it.

⚡
Why is Lenz's Law a Consequence of Energy Conservation?
When a magnet's North pole approaches a coil, induced current flows counter-clockwise, forming a North pole to repel the approaching magnet. An external agent must do mechanical work to push the magnet against this repulsive magnetic force. This mechanical work is precisely converted into electrical energy. If induced current instead attracted the magnet, infinite kinetic energy would be created from nothing, violating energy conservation!

8-9 Zero-Order Reaction Kinetics

t½ = [R]₀ / (2k)   ⇒   t½ ∝ [R]₀ (Directly proportional to initial concentration!)

Transition State

For an effective chemical reaction, reactant molecules must satisfy TWO criteria simultaneously:
1. Energy Barrier: Molecules must possess minimum energy equal to or exceeding Threshold Energy (Activation Energy Ea = E_threshold - E_reactants).
2. Orientation Barrier: Molecules must collide with proper spatial orientation (steric factor P: k = P · Z_AB · e^(-Ea/RT)).

Role of Catalyst:
• Provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy barrier.
• Does NOT alter ΔH (enthalpy of reaction)!
• Does NOT alter Equilibrium Constant (K_eq); accelerates forward and backward rates equally!

1-9 Coordination Compounds & Ligand Classifications

Inorganic Chemistry • Ch. 5

A coordination compound contains a central metal atom/ion surrounded by electron-pair donating ligands via coordinate covalent bonds, enclosed in square brackets [M(L)_n] (Coordination Sphere).
• Denticity of Ligands:
  • Unidentate: One donor atom (H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻, OH⁻).
  • Bidentate: Two donor atoms (Ethane-1,2-diamine en, Oxalate C₂O₄²⁻).
  • Polydentate: Multiple donor atoms (EDTA⁴⁻ is hexadentate!).
• Chelate Effect: Chelating (ring-forming) di- or polydentate ligands form complexes with exceptionally high thermodynamic stability compared to monodentate analogues.
• Ambidentate Ligands: Can coordinate through either of two different donor atoms (e.g., -NO₂⁻ nitro vs -ONO⁻ nitrito; -SCN⁻ thiocyanato vs -NCS⁻ isothiocyanato). Gives rise to Linkage Isomerism!

12-17 IUPAC Nomenclature of Coordination Complexes

Systematic Naming

Core IUPAC Rules:
1. Cation is named first in both cationic and anionic complexes.
2. Inside coordination entity, ligands are named in alphabetical order before the central metal.
3. Anionic ligands end in -o (e.g. chlorido, cyanido, oxalato); neutral ligands use special names (H₂O = aqua, NH₃ = ammine, CO = carbonyl, NO = nitrosyl).
4. Multiplicity prefixes: di, tri, tetra; or bis, tris, tetrakis for complex ligands containing numerical prefixes (e.g. tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)).
5. Oxidation state of central metal is indicated by Roman numerals in parentheses.
6. If the complex entity is an anion, the metal name ends with suffix -ate (e.g., ferrate, cobaltate, cuprate, platinate).

18-27 Isomerism: Structural & Stereoisomerism

Spatial & Structural Arrangements

A. Structural Isomerism:

• Linkage Isomerism: Ambidentate ligands ([Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]Cl₂ yellow vs [Co(NH₃)₅(ONO)]Cl₂ red).
• Coordination Isomerism: Interchange of ligands between cationic and anionic entities ([Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆] and [Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆]).
• Ionisation Isomerism: Exchange of counter ion with a ligand inside coordination sphere ([Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br red gives AgBr ppt; [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ violet gives BaSO₄ ppt).
• Solvate / Hydrate Isomerism: Water inside vs outside lattice ([Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃ violet vs [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O grey-green).

B. Stereoisomerism:

• Geometrical Isomerism: Cis-trans in square planar [MA₂B₂] (e.g., cis-platin cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is an anticancer drug; trans is inactive) and octahedral [MA₄B₂]. Facial (fac) and meridional (mer) isomers in [MA₃B₃] complexes.
• Optical Isomerism (Enantiomers): Non-superimposable mirror images. Common in octahedral complexes with bidentate ligands, e.g. [Co(en)₃]³⁺ and cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺. (trans-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺ has a plane of symmetry and is optically inactive!).

28-32 Valence Bond Theory (VBT) & Hybridisation

Bonding Models

Ligands donate lone pairs into vacant hybridised metal orbitals:
• Inner Orbital Complex: Uses (n-1)d orbitals ⟶ d²sp³ (Octahedral). Associated with strong-field ligands that force pairing of d-electrons; low-spin / diamagnetic.
• Outer Orbital Complex: Uses outer nd orbitals ⟶ sp³d² (Octahedral). Associated with weak-field ligands; high-spin / paramagnetic.
• Coordination Number 4: sp³ (Tetrahedral, e.g., [NiCl₄]²⁻ paramagnetic) vs dsp² (Square planar, e.g., [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ diamagnetic).

33-40 Crystal Field Theory (CFT) & Δo vs Δt Splitting

Electrostatic Crystal Field

Treats metal-ligand interactions as electrostatic point charges. Splitting of degenerate d-orbitals:
• Octahedral Splitting (Δo):
  • eg orbitals (dx²-y², dz²) point directly at ligands ⟶ destabilised to + 0.6 Δo.
  • t2g orbitals (dxy, dyz, dxz) point between axes ⟶ stabilised to - 0.4 Δo.
• Spectrochemical Series:

I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < EDTA⁴⁻ < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO

• High-Spin vs Low-Spin:
  • Weak-field ligand: Δo < P (Pairing energy) ⟶ electrons enter eg without pairing (High spin).
  • Strong-field ligand: Δo > P ⟶ electrons pair up in t2g first (Low spin).
• Tetrahedral Splitting (Δt): Inverted splitting (e lower, t₂ higher). Δt = (4/9) Δo. Tetrahedral complexes are always high-spin because Δt is too small to overcome pairing energy P!

3 Monohybrid Cross, Test Cross & Back Cross

Mendelian Ratios

A cross involving a single pair of contrasting characters (e.g., Pure Tall TT × Pure Dwarf tt):
• F₁ Generation: All heterozygous Tall (Tt).
• F₂ Generation (Selfing Tt × Tt):

F₂ Phenotypic Ratio = 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf (3 : 1)
F₂ Genotypic Ratio = 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (1 : 2 : 1)

• Test Cross: Cross of an individual displaying a dominant phenotype (genotype unknown: TT or Tt) with a homozygous recessive parent (tt):
  • If dominant parent is Tt ⟶ 1 Tall (Tt) : 1 Dwarf (tt) (Ratio = 1 : 1).
  • If dominant parent is TT ⟶ 100% Tall offspring.
• Every test cross is a back cross, but NOT every back cross is a test cross!

4 Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

Genetic Postulates

1. Law of Dominance: Characters are controlled by discrete units called factors (genes) which occur in pairs. In a dissimilar pair, one factor dominates (dominant allele) and the other is masked (recessive allele).

2. Law of Segregation (Purity of Gametes): During gamete formation, the two alleles of a gene pair separate/segregate from each other so that each gamete receives only one allele. Alleles do not show any blending! (Universal law without exception).

3. Law of Independent Assortment: When two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, segregation of one pair of characters is independent of the other pair during gamete formation (Applies only to unlinked genes on different chromosomes!).

5-7 Dihybrid Cross & Probability in Genetics

Punnett Squares

Cross involving two pairs of contrasting traits (Round Yellow RRYY × Wrinkled Green rryy):
• F₁: All Round Yellow (RrYy). Produces 4 types of gametes in equal ratio: RY, Ry, rY, ry (1 : 1 : 1 : 1).
• F₂ Generation (16 combinations via Reginald C. Punnett Square):

F₂ Phenotypic Ratio = 9 Round Yellow : 3 Round Green : 3 Wrinkled Yellow : 1 Wrinkled Green (9:3:3:1)
F₂ Genotypic Ratio = 1:2:1 : 2:4:2 : 1:2:1 (9 Distinct Genotypes)

• Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance: Proposed by Walter Sutton & Theodor Boveri (1902). Pointed out that chromosomal behaviour during meiosis parallels gene behaviour (both occur in pairs, segregate during gamete formation, and assort independently).

8-10 T.H. Morgan, Linkage & Genetic Mapping

Fly Room Discoveries

Thomas Hunt Morgan (Father of Experimental Genetics) chose fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) because: cultured on simple synthetic medium, life cycle ~2 weeks, single mating produces hundreds of progeny, clear male/female dimorphism, and distinct hereditary variations visible under low-power microscope!
• Linkage: Physical association of genes on the same chromosome. Complete linkage produces only parental types (0% recombinants).
• Recombination: Generation of non-parental gene combinations due to crossing over during pachytene of prophase-I:

Recombination Frequency (%) = (Number of Recombinants / Total Offspring) × 100

• Genetic Maps: Alfred Sturtevant used recombination frequency as a measure of physical distance between genes: 1% Recombination = 1 Map Unit = 1 centimorgan (cM).

11-12 Mechanisms of Sex Determination

Chromosomal Systems

1. XX – XY System (Humans & Drosophila): Male heterogamety. Female produces only X ova; male produces 50% X and 50% Y sperms. The father determines the sex of the child!
2. XX – XO System (Grasshopper): Male heterogamety. Male has only one X chromosome (XO); female has two (XX).
3. ZW – ZZ System (Birds): Female heterogamety! Female has ZW (heteromorphic); male has ZZ (homomorphic). The mother determines offspring sex!
4. Haplodiploidy (Honey Bees): Males (drones) develop parthenogenetically from unfertilised haploid eggs (n = 16) and have no father and cannot have sons! Females (queen & workers) develop from fertilised diploid eggs (2n = 32).

14-24 Mendelian & Chromosomal Disorders

Medical Genetics

A. Mendelian Disorders (Single-Gene Mutations):

• Haemophilia (Royal Disease): X-linked recessive. A single protein in the blood clotting cascade is affected; simple cut leads to non-stop bleeding. Carrier female (XʰX) transmits to 50% of sons.
• Colour Blindness: X-linked recessive defect in red or green cone photoreceptors. Much more common in males (~8%) than females (~0.4%).
• Sickle-Cell Anaemia: Autosomal recessive. Point mutation in 6th codon of β-globin gene from GAG (Glutamic acid) ⟶ GTG (Valine). Polymerizes under low O₂ into sickle-shaped RBCs.
• Phenylketonuria (PKU): Autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism. Deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase enzyme fails to convert phenylalanine to tyrosine, causing mental retardation.
• Thalassemia: Autosomal recessive quantitative defect (reduced synthesis of α or β globin chains). Contrast with Sickle-cell which is a qualitative defect!

B. Chromosomal Disorders (Aneuploidy via Non-Disjunction):

Down's Syndrome (Trisomy 21): Karyotype 47, XX,+21 or 47, XY,+21. First described by Langdon Down (1866). Short stature, furrowed tongue, broad palm with simian crease, intellectual disability.
Klinefelter's Syndrome: Karyotype 47, XXY. Sterile male, overall masculine development with feminine features (gynaecomastia), tall stature.
Turner's Syndrome: Karyotype 45, XO. Sterile female, rudimentary ovaries, lack of secondary sexual characteristics, webbed neck, short stature.

1-2 Search for Genetic Material & Landmark Experiments

Botany • Ch. 3

1. Griffith's Transformation (1928): Streptococcus pneumoniae. Smooth (S) virulent strain killed mice; Rough (R) non-virulent did not. Heat-killed S + Live R injected together killed mice! Live virulent S bacteria recovered. Proved a "transforming principle" was transferred from dead S to live R.

2. Avery, MacLeod & McCarty (1944): Discovered transforming principle was DNA. Proteases and RNases did not inhibit transformation, but DNase completely inhibited it!

3. Hershey & Chase Blender Experiment (1952): Unequivocal proof using T2 bacteriophage:
• ³²P labelled DNA (radioactivity detected in bacterial pellet).
• ³⁵S labelled protein coat (radioactivity detected in supernatant).
Proved DNA is the genetic material passed from virus to bacteria!

3-5 Structure of DNA & Watson-Crick B-DNA Model

Double Helix Geometry

• Nucleotide: Nitrogenous base + Deoxyribose sugar + Phosphate group (bonded via phosphoester bond to 5′ OH). Nucleotides polymerize via 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkages.
• Chargaff's Equimolar Rules (for dsDNA):

[A] = [T]  |  [G] = [C]  ⇒  [A + G] = [T + C]  ⇒  (A + G) / (T + C) = 1

• Watson-Crick Double Helix Parameters:
  • Two antiparallel polynucleotide chains (5′ ⟶ 3′ and 3′ ⟶ 5′).
  • A = T linked by 2 hydrogen bonds; G ≡ C linked by 3 hydrogen bonds.
  • Right-handed helix pitch = 3.4 nm; ~10 bp per turn; distance between consecutive base pairs = 0.34 nm; diameter = 2.0 nm.

6 Packaging of DNA & The Nucleosome

Chromatin Architecture

Human diploid genome contains 6.6 × 10⁹ bp, measuring 2.2 metres long, packed into a ~10⁻⁶ m nucleus!
• Nucleosome: Negatively charged DNA wrapped around a positively charged histone octamer (two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4).
• Rich in basic amino acids Lysine and Arginine (carrying positive charges on side chains).
• A typical nucleosome contains 200 bp of DNA. H1 histone seals the entry/exit linker DNA.
• Euchromatin: Loosely packed, stains light, transcriptionally active.
• Heterochromatin: Densely packed, stains dark, transcriptionally inactive!

7-10 DNA Replication & Meselson-Stahl Proof

Semiconservative Mechanism

Meselson & Stahl Experiment (1958): Proved semiconservative replication in E. coli using heavy ¹⁵N and light ¹⁴N with CsCl density gradient centrifugation:
• After Gen 1 (20 min): 100% hybrid density DNA (¹⁵N–¹⁴N).
• After Gen 2 (40 min): 50% hybrid density + 50% light density (¹⁴N–¹⁴N).

Replication Machinery:
• DNA-dependent DNA Polymerase: Highly processive; synthesizes only in the 5′ ⟶ 3′ direction.
• Leading Strand: Continuous synthesis (template 3′ ⟶ 5′).
• Lagging Strand: Discontinuous synthesis producing Okazaki fragments (joined together by DNA ligase).

11-17 Transcription & Eukaryotic RNA Processing

Gene Expression

Transcription Unit: Promoter (binding site for RNA polymerase), Structural gene, and Terminator.
• Template Strand: 3′ ⟶ 5′ polarity.
• Coding Strand: 5′ ⟶ 3′ polarity (sequence identical to mRNA except T replaced by U).

Eukaryotic RNA Polymerases:
• RNA Polymerase I: Transcribes 28S, 18S, and 5.8S rRNAs.
• RNA Polymerase II: Transcribes precursor of mRNA (heterogeneous nuclear RNA, hnRNA).
• RNA Polymerase III: Transcribes tRNA, 5S rRNA, and snRNAs.

Post-Transcriptional Modifications of hnRNA:
• 5′ Capping: Addition of methyl guanosine triphosphate.
• 3′ Polyadenylation (Tailing): 200–300 adenylate residues added.
• Splicing: Non-coding intervening sequences (introns) removed; coding sequences (exons) joined by spliceosome!

18-24 The Genetic Code & Translation

Ribosomal Protein Synthesis

Salient Features of Genetic Code (George Gamow, Nirenberg, Khorana):
• Triplet: 61 sense codons code for 20 amino acids; 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) code for NO amino acid.
• Unambiguous & Specific: One codon codes for only one amino acid.
• Degenerate: Some amino acids are specified by more than one codon (except Methionine AUG and Tryptophan UGG).
• Universal: AUG codes for Methionine from bacteria to humans!
• Dual Function of AUG: Acts as initiation/start codon AND codes for Methionine.

• tRNA (Adaptor Molecule): Has amino acid acceptor end (CCA-3′) and anticodon loop that pairs complementarily with mRNA codon.
• Ribosomes: Prokaryotic 70S (50S + 30S) & Eukaryotic 80S (60S + 40S). 23S rRNA in bacteria acts as peptidyl transferase ribozyme!

25-28 Regulation of Gene Expression: The Lac Operon

Inducible Model (Jacob & Monod)

Consists of: Regulatory gene i (inhibitor/repressor), Promoter p, Operator o, and three structural genes z, y, a:
• z gene: β-galactosidase (hydrolyses lactose into galactose + glucose).
• y gene: Permease (increases cell permeability to β-galactosides).
• a gene: Transacetylase.

• In absence of inducer (Lactose): Repressor binds operator, blocking RNA polymerase ⟶ Operon OFF.
• In presence of inducer: Lactose/allolactose binds repressor, inactivating it. RNA pol transcribes z, y, a ⟶ Operon ON.

21-23 Hardy-Weinberg Principle & Population Genetics

Equilibrium Mathematics

In a large, randomly mating diploid population without evolutionary disturbances, allele frequencies remain constant from generation to generation:

p + q = 1   and   p² + 2pq + q² = 1

where p = frequency of dominant allele A, q = frequency of recessive allele a, p² = homozygous dominant (AA), 2pq = heterozygous (Aa), and q² = homozygous recessive (aa).

Five Factors That Disrupt Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium:
1. Gene Migration / Gene Flow (alleles enter/leave via migration).
2. Genetic Drift (random allele frequency changes in small populations; e.g. Founder Effect & Bottleneck Effect).
3. Mutation (creates novel genetic variations).
4. Genetic Recombination (shuffles alleles during crossing over).
5. Natural Selection.

24 Types of Natural Selection

Population Curve Shifts

• Stabilising Selection: Favours intermediate mean phenotypes; narrows the distribution peak (e.g., human birth weight ~3 kg survives best).
• Directional Selection: Favours one extreme phenotype; shifts peak in one direction (e.g., Industrial Melanism in peppered moth Biston betularia — dark melanic moths favoured in soot-covered polluted areas; DDT resistance in mosquitoes).
• Disruptive Selection: Favours both extreme phenotypes; selects against intermediate mean, creating two distinct peaks.
